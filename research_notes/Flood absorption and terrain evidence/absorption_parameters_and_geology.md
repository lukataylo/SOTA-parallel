## (a) Amenity grass / lawn on London Clay and on made ground: infiltration rate and storage

### Takeaway
No UK design guide gives a "grass on London Clay" infiltration rate; the defensible approach is to treat the subgrade as C753 "no infiltration" class (k below about 1e-8 m/s, under 0.04 mm/h) and credit grass only with topsoil and depression storage, not sustained infiltration. Measured UK evidence (Manchester) shows grass plots eliminate nearly all runoff in ordinary rain, but that was not a cloudburst test.

### Cited Findings
- C753 Table 20.1 classes subgrades by coefficient of permeability: 1e-6 to 1e-3 m/s total infiltration; 1e-8 to 1e-6 m/s partial infiltration; 1e-10 to 1e-8 m/s no infiltration. Note 1: partial infiltration may be used below 1e-8 m/s "but the infiltration of water is not allowed for in the storage design". [DESIGN, read in primary text, p.397] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20 (Marshalls-hosted copy)](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- In-situ Guelph permeameter tests in a London Clay cutting gave roughly 3e-11 to 4.5e-8 m/s over three tests; values of about 5e-9 m/s at a Newbury cutting were attributed to near-surface weathering, with K falling with depth in unweathered clay. [MEASURED, search snippet only] — [Dixon et al., In situ measurements of near-surface hydraulic conductivity in engineered clay slopes (NORA)](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/522530/1/Dixon%20et%20al%20Hydraulic%20Conductivity%20REVISED%20paper%20for%20QJEGH%2010%205%2018.pdf)
- Manchester plot experiment (9 m2 plots of grass, asphalt, and asphalt with a tree): grass "almost totally eliminated surface runoff". [MEASURED, UK, natural rainfall; abstract via search snippet] — [Armson, Stringer & Ennos 2013, Urban Forestry & Urban Greening 12(3) 282-286](https://owl.cwp.org/mdocs-posts/the-effect-of-street-trees-and-amenity-grass-on-urban-surface-water-runoff-in-manchester-uk/)
- A presentation by Ennos reports the same experiment as grass reducing runoff by 99 %. [MEASURED, secondary, search snippet only] — [Mersey Forest UCF steering group presentations 2015](https://www.merseyforest.org.uk/files/documents/1328/UCF+Steering+Group+-+combined+presentations+-+19+Mar+2015.pdf)
- Compaction strongly reduces infiltration of urban soils: Florida double-ring tests found compacted urban (sandy) soils at 8 to 175 mm/h, far below uncompacted rates. [MEASURED, non-UK, sandy soil, search snippet only] — [Gregory et al., JSWC compaction article](https://abe.ufl.edu/faculty/mdukes/pdf/stormwater/Gregor-et-%20al-JSWC-compaction-article.pdf)
- TfL's guide warns that compaction during construction "can negatively impact infiltration rates" and that soil management is fundamental to SuDS function. [Qualitative guidance, read in primary text, s.2.5 and s.3.11] — [SuDS in London: a guide (TfL, 2016)](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/sustainable-urban-drainage-november-2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- [DERIVED] London Clay at 3e-11 to 4.5e-8 m/s is 0.0001 to 0.16 mm/h. In a 40-minute storm that is at most about 0.1 mm of infiltration into the clay itself, i.e. zero for modelling purposes. Any absorption credit for grass on clay must come from the topsoil pore space and surface depressions.
- For grass on made ground over alluvium (the actual condition at Blackfriars Road, see geology section), permeability is site-specific and unknown; made ground is heterogeneous and often compacted. A conservative model would use the partial-infiltration class (0.036 to 3.6 mm/h) rather than a gravel rate.
- The Armson result (about 99 % reduction) reflects ordinary Manchester rainfall on small plots with imported topsoil; it should not be quoted as the capture fraction in a 60 to 120 mm/h cloudburst, where rainfall intensity exceeds the infiltration capacity of fine or compacted soils.

### Gaps
- No sourced UK value found for (i) steady infiltration rate of amenity turf on London Clay topsoil in mm/h, or (ii) depression/surface storage depth for mown grass in mm. Values commonly used in models (for example a few mm of depression storage and Green-Ampt or Horton parameters for clay loam) were not verified in this session and should be cited from the model's own documentation.
- No infiltration test data (BRE 365 or infiltrometer) found for any green space inside the 512 m study square.

## (b) Rain gardens / bioretention cells

### Takeaway
UK design values are: filter medium saturated conductivity 100 to 300 mm/h, surface ponding 150 to 300 mm, filter depth roughly 300 to 800 mm, and a cell area of a few percent of the area drained. Sources disagree on the area ratio (2 to 4 % versus 5 to 10 % versus a 10:1 loading ratio).

### Cited Findings
- C753 guideline limits for bioretention fill media saturated hydraulic conductivity are 100 mm/h (lower) and 300 mm/h (upper); the paper labels its model configurations "C100 and C300 ... CIRIA guidance values at the lower and upper permissible limits for Ks". [DESIGN, peer-reviewed quote of C753, read in primary text, Table 2 p.6] — [De-Ville et al. 2021, Water 13(15) 2014 (White Rose copy)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/182241/1/water-13-02014.pdf)
- The same study modelled UK roadside cells as 1 m2 of cell receiving 9 m2 impermeable area (10:1 loading ratio), 100 mm allowable ponding, 700 mm fill media, 300 mm drainage layer, under 1-hour design storms for Newcastle with 10, 30 and 100-year return periods at a 1-minute time step. [Modelled, UK, read in primary text] — [De-Ville et al. 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/182241/1/water-13-02014.pdf)
- The recycled test medium had lab Ks = 101 mm/h and porosity 44 %, both within recommended ranges. Field-scale studies report lower Ks than lab and pilot scale. [MEASURED, lab, UK] — [De-Ville et al. 2021](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/182241/1/water-13-02014.pdf)
- Temporary surface storage above the filter medium: "normally ... a maximum depth of 150-300mm", citing C753 Fig 18.1; overflow set 150 to 300 mm above the surface. [DESIGN, read in primary text, p.2-3] — [United Utilities s104 SuDS guidance: bio-retention](https://cumbria.unitedutilities.com/globalassets/documents/builders--developers-docs/s104-suds-guidance-document-bio-retention.v1.pdf)
- A bioretention component "cannot serve more than 0.8 Ha or 2-4% of the overall site area to be drained", sized with C753 Eq 18.1 (s.18.4.1); forebay at least 10 % of the component. [RULE citing C753, read in primary text, p.1-2; the "2-4 %" wording is ambiguous but is normally read as cell area being 2 to 4 % of the drained area] — [United Utilities s104 guidance](https://cumbria.unitedutilities.com/globalassets/documents/builders--developers-docs/s104-suds-guidance-document-bio-retention.v1.pdf)
- Manchester's highways guide (based on C753) suggests rain garden surface area of 5 to 10 % of the total catchment and filter medium depth of 300 to 800 mm. [RULE, search snippet only; the PDF returned HTTP 403] — [Manchester City Council Highways SuDS developer design guide](https://www.manchester.gov.uk/download/downloads/id/29061/highways_suds_-_developer_design_guide.pdf)
- TfL: "bioretention systems can deal with only small catchment areas and are likely to be overwhelmed during heavy storms. The design should therefore allow for contingency flow paths and/or provide outfall." [Qualitative, read in primary text, s.3.8 p.48] — [SuDS in London: a guide](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/sustainable-urban-drainage-november-2016.pdf)
- Australian FAWB / Water Sensitive Cities guidance prescribes the same 100 to 300 mm/h range for temperate climates; Hong Kong prefers 50 to 200 mm/h. [DESIGN, non-UK, search snippet only] — [FAWB guideline specifications for soil media in bioretention systems](https://watersensitivecities.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/guideline-specs-soil-media-july2006.pdf)

### Inferences
- [DERIVED] In a short storm the binding constraint is the cell's storage plus what the medium can pass, scaled by the area ratio. Example at a 10:1 loading ratio: 150 mm ponding absorbs the first 15 mm of catchment rainfall; the medium at 100 mm/h passes 50 mm of water per m2 of cell in 30 minutes, equal to a further 5 mm of catchment rainfall (provided the underdrain or sub-storage can accept it). At a 2 to 4 % area ratio, 150 to 300 mm of ponding equals only 3 to 12 mm of catchment rainfall. So a rain garden absorbs roughly the first 5 to 20 mm of a cloudburst from its catchment and overflows after that.
- On clay or lined cells the exfiltration to subsoil is zero within the event; the water passing the medium goes to the drainage layer and underdrain, so it is delayed and attenuated rather than removed. De-Ville et al. modelled exactly this lined case.
- [DERIVED] Sub-surface storage in the cell: 700 mm of medium at 44 % porosity is up to about 300 mm of pore space if fully dry, but only the drainable fraction (porosity minus field capacity) is available; this was not quantified in the sources read.

### Gaps
- C753 chapter 18 not read directly; filter depth, drain-down time and Eq 18.1 are quoted via secondary UK documents only.
- No UK field measurement of rain garden performance in a greater-than-30-year short-duration storm was found; the paper itself notes monitoring periods are usually too short to show extreme-event performance.

## (c) Street tree pits, including engineered pits (Stockholm structural soil, cell systems)

### Takeaway
Engineered tree pits store water in the voids of a stone skeleton: UK supplier guidance gives 26 % void space and 8 to 45 m3 of structural soil per tree (Stockholm city requires at least 15 m3). Measured UK evidence is that a tree plus its pit cut runoff from small asphalt plots by up to 62 %, mostly through infiltration into the pit, not canopy interception.

### Cited Findings
- "The compacted stone and soil mix has 26% void space for the storage and dispersal of air and water." [RULE, UK supplier, read in primary text, p.9] — [Stockholm Tree Pits, Tree pits with structural soils practice note v1.4](https://stockholmtreepits.co.uk/assets/downloads/tree-pits-with-structural-soils-practice-note-V1-4.pdf)
- Recommended structural soil volume by mature tree size: very small (<5 m) 8 m3 (6 if shared); small (5-10 m) 15 m3 (12 shared); medium (10-15 m) 26 m3 (20 shared); large (15-25 m) 36 m3 (28 shared); massive (>25 m) 45 m3 (35 shared). Stone 40-75 mm under roads; top 200 mm clean stone 20-40 mm as the air/water distribution layer. [RULE, UK supplier, read in primary text, p.8-10] — [Stockholm Tree Pits practice note](https://stockholmtreepits.co.uk/assets/downloads/tree-pits-with-structural-soils-practice-note-V1-4.pdf)
- The same supplier's website lists different minimum volumes (12, 24, 40 ... up to 72 m3). [RULE, search snippet only; conflicts with the practice note table] — [stockholmtreepits.co.uk](https://stockholmtreepits.co.uk/)
- City of Stockholm: "the volume of the Stockholm structural soil must be at least 15 m3 per tree" and the bed must be continuous; build-up includes a 150 mm aeration layer of 32/63 mm macadam over 600 mm of 90/150 mm stone with substrate or biochar flushed into the cavities; biochar-macadam uses 32/90 mm stone plus 15 to 25 % by volume biochar/compost. [DESIGN, Sweden, read in primary text, p.8-18] — [Planting beds in the City of Stockholm: a handbook (TDAG-hosted)](https://www.tdag.org.uk/uploads/4/2/8/0/4280686/planting_beds_in_the_city_of_stockholm_v4.5.pdf)
- A case study in the TfL guide describes an 800 mm thick boulder-based structural soil "60% of which is cavities" before mulch was washed into the voids. [Case study, non-UK plaza, read in primary text, p.54] — [SuDS in London: a guide](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/sustainable-urban-drainage-november-2016.pdf)
- Minnesota's comparison table assumes 92 % void space for Silva Cell crate systems and a 20 % soil component for rock-based structural soil. [RULE, US, search snippet only] — [Minnesota Stormwater Manual, comparison of tree techniques](https://stormwater.pca.state.mn.us/comparison_of_different_tree_techniques)
- Manchester: trees and their tree pits reduced runoff from asphalt plots "by as much as 62%"; authors attribute the effect to infiltration into the tree pit. [MEASURED, UK, 9 m2 plots, natural rainfall; abstract via search snippet] — [Armson, Stringer & Ennos 2013](https://owl.cwp.org/mdocs-posts/the-effect-of-street-trees-and-amenity-grass-on-urban-surface-water-runoff-in-manchester-uk/)
- Howard Street, Salford SuDS-enabled tree trench monitoring: average peak flow attenuation 81 %, average volume reaching the sewer reduced by 78 %, average peak delay 68 minutes. The same fact file states "A normal tree can contain 43% of rainfall; a SuDS-enabled street tree can retain 78%". [MEASURED, UK, averaged over monitored events, read in primary text] — [GMCA IGNITION fact file: SuDS-enabled trees](https://www.greatermanchester-ca.gov.uk/media/4138/ignition-fact-files-suds-enabled-trees_finaldoc.pdf)
- A Swedish study of novel street-tree installations concluded that waterlogging must be avoided by an underdrain or by limiting use to soils with sufficient exfiltration, and that "it remains to be determined if tree pits may achieve meaningful reductions in stormwater runoff". [Peer-reviewed opinion, Sweden, search snippet only] — [Characterizing the belowground environment in novel street tree installations in Sweden](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346911158_Characterizing_the_Belowground_Environment_in_Novel_Street_Tree_Installations_in_Sweden)
- TfL: "the rate of infiltration of a tree pit dictates the size of the tree pit required for water storage"; attenuation or infiltration areas can seldom be created around existing trees. [Qualitative, read in primary text, s.3.9 p.51-53] — [SuDS in London: a guide](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/sustainable-urban-drainage-november-2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- [DERIVED] Upper-bound void storage per tree = structural soil volume x 26 %: 15 m3 gives about 3.9 m3 (3,900 L); 26 m3 gives about 6.8 m3; 36 m3 gives about 9.4 m3. A 3.9 m3 pit would hold 50 mm of rain from roughly 78 m2 of paving if all voids were empty, air could escape, and inlets could deliver the flow. Real available storage is lower (residual moisture, roots, inlet capacity). Label this as an upper bound.
- For crate/cell systems (about 92 % void) the same excavation holds roughly 3.5 times more water than stone structural soil.
- In a cloudburst the limiting factor is usually the inlet (kerb inlet or gully-fed distribution pipe), not the void volume; none of the sources read gives an inlet capacity in L/s.
- Sources disagree on void fraction for stone-based systems (26 % UK supplier; 60 % for an unfilled boulder skeleton; about 20 % soil fraction in US guidance). 26 % is the only figure stated for a finished UK Stockholm-type mix.
- The 62 % (Armson) and 78 to 81 % (Salford) figures are for ordinary rainfall and should not be transferred unchanged to a 1-in-30 or rarer short storm.

### Gaps
- C753 chapter 19 (trees) not read; its soil-volume and storage guidance is not quoted here.
- No published litres-per-tree storm storage figure from Stockholm or UK guidance was found; the per-tree volumes above are my multiplication.
- No UK monitoring of a tree pit during a named cloudburst event was found.

## (d) Permeable paving

### Takeaway
C753 is explicit: a new pervious surface should pass at least 2500 mm/h, and design should apply a factor of safety of 10 for clogging, giving a long-term minimum of 250 mm/h, which still exceeds any UK design rainfall intensity. Storage, not surface infiltration, is the limit: sub-base porosity is normally at least 30 %, and run-on from adjacent impermeable surface is limited to 2:1.

### Cited Findings
- "A minimum value of 2500 mm/h (for new pavements) is considered reasonable for a pavement surface to be considered pervious". [DESIGN, read in primary text, s.20.5.1 p.400] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- "It is recommended that a factor of safety of 10 is applied to the surface infiltration rate of all surface types, to allow for clogging ... (ie the long-term surface infiltration rate will be a minimum of 250 mm/h)." Clogging very rarely seals the whole surface. [DESIGN, read in primary text, s.20.5.1 p.400] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- "The surface infiltration capacity has no relationship to the infiltration capacity of the soils below the pavement construction." [DESIGN note, read in primary text, p.400] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- Sub-base "should have a minimum porosity that is consistent with the design calculations (normally at least 30%)" and minimum permeability 6 x 10^-2 m/s. Porous concrete surface rate minimum 250 mm/h by ASTM C1701; hydraulically bound coarse aggregate minimum 10,000 mm/h. [DESIGN, read in primary text, s.20.11.4 p.422 and tables p.415-425] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- Attenuation storage volume = volume of sub-base x porosity (C753 s.20.5.3). Where adjacent areas drain onto the surface "the maximum ratio should be 2:1 (impermeable:pervious)" to limit silt clogging; Kellagher (2013) analysis shows the sub-base can typically serve at least twice the pavement area under tight throttles and nearly three times when the throttle exceeds 5 l/s/ha. [DESIGN, read in primary text, p.394, p.402] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- Pavement type by subgrade: Type A total infiltration needs k of 1e-6 to 1e-3 m/s; Type B partial 1e-8 to 1e-6; Type C (lined, no infiltration) 1e-10 to 1e-8; highest water level within 1000 mm of formation rules out Types A and B. [DESIGN, read in primary text, Table 20.1 p.397] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- UK field tests of unmaintained concrete block permeable paving (ASTM C1781) gave a fitted decline of y = 979.59 e^(-0.076x) mm/h with x = age in years; all sites still coped with likely UK rainfall. [MEASURED, UK, trade press report of Interpave-linked testing, search snippet only] — [pavingexpert.com, CBPP and the clogging myth](https://www.pavingexpert.com/about-us/news001/news430)
- Field permeable concrete pavers ranged from 125 to 25,000 mm/h; the rate exceeded in 90 % of cases was about 700 mm/h (400 mm/h from Lucke et al. data). [MEASURED, non-UK, search snippet only] — [Evaluating the field performance of permeable concrete pavers, Water 14(14) 2143](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/14/2143)
- One pavement gave a median 122 mm/h by one test method versus 332 to 373 mm/h by two others; lower parts of pavements and areas receiving run-on show lower rates. [MEASURED, search snippet only] — [Lucke et al., Urban, Planning and Transport Research 2014](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21650020.2014.893200)
- A manufacturer states newly installed block paving exceeds 4,000 mm/h and quotes 400 mm/h as a required rate, without a source. [RULE, supplier, search snippet only] — [Tobermore](https://www.tobermore.co.uk/professional/blog/what-is-the-infiltration-rate-of-permeable-paving/)

### Inferences
- [DERIVED] Sub-base storage depth in mm of water = thickness x 0.30: 150 mm gives 45 mm; 250 mm gives 75 mm; 350 mm gives 105 mm; 450 mm gives 135 mm. With 2:1 run-on (the pavement serving three times its own area) a 350 mm sub-base holds about 35 mm of rain over the whole served area before relying on the outlet.
- The sources disagree on the long-term surface rate: C753 design minimum 250 mm/h; UK decay curve implies about 460 mm/h at 10 years and about 210 mm/h at 20 years (my evaluation of the quoted formula); field 10th percentile 400 to 700 mm/h; individual clogged patches fall to about 120 mm/h or lower. All of these exceed a 120 mm/h cloudburst only if run-on is limited; with 2:1 run-on the effective demand is three times the rainfall intensity (360 mm/h in a 120 mm/h burst), which a clogged surface at 250 mm/h would not pass.
- On the low-permeability ground expected here, paving would be Type C (lined) or Type B with infiltration not counted, so in the model it is storage with a throttled outlet to the combined sewer, not a loss term.

### Gaps
- C753 typical sub-base thickness tables for hydraulic storage were not extracted; storage depths above are computed, not quoted.
- The UK decay-curve data was seen only as a search snippet of a trade article; the underlying Interpave report was not read.

## Canopy interception by street trees in a single intense event

### Takeaway
Canopy storage is of the order of 1 mm per event, filled in the first minutes of rain, so in a 20 to 120 mm cloudburst it removes a few percent at most and does not reduce the peak. Annual interception percentages must not be applied to a single intense storm.

### Cited Findings
- Rainfall-simulator measurements on 20 urban tree species: mean surface saturation storage 0.86 mm (SD 0.11 mm), from 0.59 mm (crape myrtle) to 1.81 mm (blue spruce), tested at 12 intensities from 3.5 to 139.5 mm/h. [MEASURED, lab, California] — [Xiao & McPherson 2016, J. Environ. Qual. 45:188-198 (USDA Treesearch)](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/50394)
- Paired-catchment removal of street trees (green ash and Norway maple, Fond du Lac, Wisconsin): runoff volume rose by 198 m3, 4 % of measured runoff; "peak discharge was generally not affected by tree removal"; 66 L per m2 of canopy (6.6 cm) is the cumulative loss "over the 42 storms" of May to September 2020, and it lumps interception, transpiration and infiltration changes. [MEASURED, US, read in primary text, p.1 and p.7] — [Selbig et al. 2022, Science of the Total Environment 806:151296](https://fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/jrnl/2022/nrs_2022_selbig_001.pdf)
- The same paper reports "a diminished capacity for canopy interception for larger storms" and cites Xiao and McPherson (2002) that little interception occurs once surface storage is taken up, which happens proportionally sooner in a 25-year storm. [Read in primary text, p.6] — [Selbig et al. 2022](https://fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/jrnl/2022/nrs_2022_selbig_001.pdf)
- Mechanism stated by the authors: changes to interception and infiltration "are greatest early in the storm, while the storm hydrograph is just beginning to rise"; once leaves are wet and canopy storage is full, a later rainfall peak passes through. Canopy can delay throughfall but this "does not affect the peak in net precipitation" (Asadian 2010, as cited). [Read in primary text, p.7-8] — [Selbig et al. 2022](https://fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/jrnl/2022/nrs_2022_selbig_001.pdf)
- A study of Norway maple and small-leaved lime reported about 38 % of gross precipitation intercepted. This is a seasonal/aggregate share, not an event depth. [MEASURED, aggregate, search snippet only] — [HESS 23, 3865 (2019)](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/23/3865/2019/)
- London i-Tree Eco: London's canopy gives an avoided runoff of 3.4 million m3 per year. This is an annual city-wide figure. [Modelled, annual, read in primary text, p.52] — [SuDS in London: a guide](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/sustainable-urban-drainage-november-2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- [DERIVED] 66 L/m2 over 42 storms is about 1.6 mm per storm per m2 of canopy on average, consistent in order of magnitude with the roughly 1 mm lab storage figure.
- [DERIVED] At about 1 to 2 mm per event under the canopy, interception is 2.5 to 5 % of a 40 mm storm and about 1 to 2 % of an 80 mm storm, and only over the canopy footprint. With, say, 10 to 20 % canopy cover over a street the area-averaged loss is a few tenths of a millimetre. For a flood model it is reasonable to represent canopy as an initial loss of about 1 mm over canopy cells, or to ignore it.
- The 38 % and 43 % ("a normal tree can contain 43 % of rainfall") figures are dominated by many small events and evaporation between them; they are the type of number the brief warns against using for a cloudburst.
- Caveat on the Xiao and McPherson number: the abstract does not say whether 0.86 mm is per unit leaf surface or per unit crown projection; if per unit leaf area, canopy storage per unit ground area would be higher by roughly the leaf area index. Even then it would be a few mm, which does not change the conclusion.

### Gaps
- No UK measurement of per-event canopy interception in a short high-intensity storm was found.
- The basis (leaf area versus projected crown area) of the 0.86 mm figure was not confirmed from the full paper.

## Manning's n for overland sheet flow: short grass versus asphalt/concrete

### Takeaway
The standard NRCS TR-55 sheet-flow table gives n = 0.011 for smooth surfaces (concrete, asphalt) and 0.15 for short-grass prairie (0.24 dense grass), a roughly 14 to 22-fold difference, but these are valid only for very shallow sheet flow over short lengths.

### Cited Findings
- TR-55 sheet-flow roughness (reproduced as TxDOT Table 4-6, source NRCS 1986): smooth surfaces (concrete, asphalt, gravel, bare soil) 0.011; fallow 0.05; short grass prairie 0.15; dense grasses 0.24; Bermuda grass 0.41; range 0.13; woods with light underbrush 0.40; dense underbrush 0.80. [DESIGN table, US] — [TxDOT Hydraulic Design Manual, sheet flow time calculation](https://www.txdot.gov/manuals/des/hyd/chapter-4--hydrology/section-11--time-of-concentration/sheet-flow-time-calculation.html)
- Stated limits: maximum sheet flow length 100 ft, and "'n' values for overland flows are not to be used in other channel or floodplain applications". — [TxDOT Hydraulic Design Manual](https://www.txdot.gov/manuals/des/hyd/chapter-4--hydrology/section-11--time-of-concentration/sheet-flow-time-calculation.html)
- The TR-55 values are described as a composite of information compiled by Engman (1986). [Secondary attribution, search snippet only] — [Bentley SewerCAD help, Manning's n coefficients](https://DOCS.BENTLEY.COM/LiveContent/web/Bentley%20SewerCAD%20SS5-v1/en/GUID-3860B0C0545A4143A8B066C63DAF73E7.html)
- For concentrated gutter flow the same Bentley table lists asphalt pavement at 0.013 (smooth) and 0.016 (rough); these are not sheet-flow values. [DESIGN table, search snippet only] — [Bentley SewerCAD help](https://DOCS.BENTLEY.COM/LiveContent/web/Bentley%20SewerCAD%20SS5-v1/en/GUID-3860B0C0545A4143A8B066C63DAF73E7.html)

### Inferences
- The sheet-flow values apply where flow depth is small relative to the grass blades (a few cm at most). Once water is deep enough to submerge mown grass, as on a flood pathway, the effective roughness falls well below 0.15, so applying 0.15 to deep flow in a 2D model would overstate the slowing effect of grass.
- For a 2D surface-water model the honest statement for a slide is: grass is about an order of magnitude rougher than asphalt for shallow sheet flow (0.15 to 0.24 versus 0.011), and the contrast narrows as depth increases.

### Gaps
- The original Engman (1986) paper and TR-55 Table 3-1 were not read directly; values come from a state DOT reproduction.
- UK 2D-modelling roughness conventions (for example the values used in Environment Agency surface-water mapping for roads versus green space) were not retrieved in this session and should be sourced separately if the model follows them.

## Superficial geology, soil and groundwater under Blackfriars Road / Southwark station / The Cut, and what infiltration capacity that implies

### Takeaway
The area is reclaimed Thames floodplain: made ground (several metres) over Holocene alluvium with peat (the "Bankside Channel" palaeochannel runs from Waterloo towards Bankside), over late-glacial river terrace gravel, over London Clay; shallow groundwater sits in the gravel a few metres down. The council's SFRA says infiltration SuDS has limited compatibility across most of the borough and that features should be storage-led and lined; no site-specific infiltration test for the study square was found.

### Cited Findings
- BGS records London Clay Formation as bedrock around Blackfriars Road, overlain by superficial alluvium (clay, silt, sand and peat). The BGS summary omits late-glacial river terrace gravels, which are "mapped extensively from borehole records in the area" and attributed to the Shepperton Gravel (Gibbard 1994). [Desk study citing BGS, read in primary text, s.3.1] — [PCA, 216-220 Blackfriars Road SE1 8NL, geoarchaeological WSI, Dec 2023](https://docs.planning.org.uk/20240124/54/S7O39GKB00U00/fwqejl1ciohtyqz9.pdf)
- 216-220 Blackfriars Road sits at the northern margin of the Bankside Channel, a palaeochannel running SW-NE "from the area around Waterloo towards Bankside". Three boreholes (Sept 2023) found Lower Alluvium, Peat and Upper Alluvium over Pleistocene gravels; "all five test pits did not penetrate the Made Ground". [MEASURED, site boreholes, read in primary text, s.2.6-2.8, 3.1.3] — [PCA 216-220 Blackfriars Road WSI](https://docs.planning.org.uk/20240124/54/S7O39GKB00U00/fwqejl1ciohtyqz9.pdf)
- Channel fill is described (Young 2018) as Holocene sediments including "at most sites a bed of peat" resting on the gravel or separated by organic sand or silt, overlain by less organic alluvial silts. [Read in primary text, s.3.1.4] — [PCA 216-220 Blackfriars Road WSI](https://docs.planning.org.uk/20240124/54/S7O39GKB00U00/fwqejl1ciohtyqz9.pdf)
- Analogue site about 1.5 km east (74-84 Long Lane SE1; note this is outside the study square): BGS boreholes within 100 m show made ground averaging 3.7 m thick; Kempton Park Gravel Formation with top at 1.7 to -0.1 m OD and average thickness 4.4 m; London Clay top at 7.4 to 8.7 m bgl (-3.4 to -5.5 m OD), 13.7 m thick; alluvium present in one of six logs. Perched groundwater in the gravel in two of five boreholes at 3.4 to 4.4 m bgl. The gravel is a Secondary A aquifer; London Clay is unproductive strata; groundwater flooding risk from the gravel over clay is flagged. [MEASURED, BGS logs as tabulated, read in primary text, s.4.2] — [CGL, 74-84 Long Lane Phase 1 geoenvironmental desk study](https://docs.planning.org.uk/20220719/54/REWRM2KB00300/8o24hfsub2lnt6sl.pdf)
- A Southwark committee report for 55 Southwark Street cites BGS boreholes with groundwater 2.8 m below ground level. [Search snippet only; nearby but not in the study square] — [Southwark Council report, 55 Southwark Street](https://moderngov.southwark.gov.uk/documents/s56762/Report%2055%20Southwark%20Street%20London%20SE1%201TE.pdf)
- Documents for 41-45 Blackfriars Road conflict: one assessment reports no groundwater found, an audit places groundwater at about 1 m below ground level, and another says the site is founded in London Clay. [Search snippets only; these are hosted on Camden's system and may relate to a different "Blackfriars Road"-named file, so low confidence] — [Campbell Reith audit, 41-45 Blackfriars Road](https://camdocs.camden.gov.uk/CMWebDrawer/Record/5519566/file/document?inline=)
- Southwark SFRA: superficial deposits (River Terrace Deposits, Alluvium, Peat, Head, Langley Silt and others) "blanket much of the northern half" of the borough; London Clay dominates bedrock in the south and north-west. [Read in extracted text, s.1.3.5, p.13] — [LB Southwark SFRA, January 2017](https://www.southwark.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2024-05/southwark_council_sfra.pdf)

Section 5.3: "The majority of the Borough has been identified as potentially unsuitable for infiltration SuDS owing to the impermeable London Clay Formation. Where the London Clay Formation is absent or where River Terrace Deposits exist, enhanced site investigation is required"; and "selected SuDS features will need to be focused on surface water storage and attenuation, and appropriately lined". The BGS-based map has four classes: highly suitable, probably suitable, potentially suitable for bespoke designs, unlikely to be suitable. - Neighbouring Lambeth states its clay soil "prevents the use of infiltrating SuDS", leaving the borough reliant on the combined sewer. [Borough statement, search snippet only; applies to Lambeth generally, the borough boundary is just west of the study square] — [Lambeth, What are SuDS](https://haveyoursay.lambeth.gov.uk/en-GB/projects/what-are-suds/1)
- The TfL guide lists high groundwater as a constraint: it can enter the component and reduce storage, and "high levels of groundwater can reduce the infiltration rate of SuDS features". [Read in primary text, s.2.4 p.20] — [SuDS in London: a guide](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/sustainable-urban-drainage-november-2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- Likely sequence under the study square, top down: made ground (order 2 to 4 m, not bottomed in trial pits at 216-220 Blackfriars Road); alluvial silt/clay with peat where the Bankside Channel crosses (it runs Waterloo to Bankside, so it plausibly passes under or near The Cut and Southwark station, but the exact line was not checked on a map); river terrace sand and gravel (a few metres); London Clay from very roughly 7 to 9 m below ground. Depths are transferred from the Long Lane analogue and are indicative only.
- Naming disagreement to flag: BGS mapping and geoenvironmental reports call the gravel Kempton Park Gravel; the geoarchaeological literature assigns the gravel beneath the floodplain alluvium to the younger Shepperton Gravel. For hydrology the distinction does not matter (both are permeable sand and gravel); for a slide, "river terrace gravel (Kempton Park / Shepperton Gravel)" is safe.
- Infiltration capacity implied: the surface layers that a rain garden or tree pit would actually sit in are made ground and alluvial silt/clay/peat, which are low permeability and heterogeneous; the permeable gravel is below them and holds perched groundwater at about 3 to 4.5 m bgl (2.8 m at Southwark Street). So the gravel's high permeability is not directly usable by shallow features without excavating through made ground and alluvium, and the 1 m clearance to groundwater plus made-ground contamination and proximity to buildings, basements and the Jubilee line/utility corridor are further constraints. On C753 Table 20.1 this points to Type B (partial) at best and commonly Type C (lined) systems.
- Feasibility verdict that the evidence supports: infiltration SuDS is not ruled out in policy for this part of Southwark, but is treated as "bespoke design, subject to site testing", and the council's own default is lined storage and attenuation discharging at a controlled rate to the combined sewer. In the flood model, absorption features here should therefore be represented mainly as finite storage volumes with slow release, not as continuous infiltration sinks.
- The two SFRA sentences ("potentially suitable ... bespoke" in s.1.3.5.3 versus "potentially unsuitable" in s.5.3) are worded in opposite directions for the same mapped class; both mean infiltration cannot be assumed.

### Gaps
- The BGS Geology Viewer, BGS infiltration SuDS map and BGS borehole scans for the exact study square (around TQ 316 800) were not queried directly; superficial geology is taken from reports that cite BGS. The class shown on SFRA Map A11 at Blackfriars Road was not read off the map.
- No borehole log or groundwater monitoring result from inside the 512 m square was retrieved (the 216-220 Blackfriars Road borehole depths are in a report I did not find; the Southwark station over-site development drainage strategy by Heyne Tillett Steel, dated 28/11/2024, exists but was not located).
- No BRE 365 soakaway test result for this area was found, and no published hydraulic conductivity for Kempton Park / Shepperton Gravel was retrieved (typical clean sand-and-gravel values would be of the order of 1e-4 to 1e-3 m/s, but that is unsourced here).
- Thames Water material on sewer capacity or infiltration feasibility for this catchment was not retrieved.

## What UK design guides tell you to assume for infiltration in a design storm, and the factor of safety

### Takeaway
UK practice is to use a site-measured infiltration rate (BRE Digest 365 soakaway test or BS EN ISO 22282 permeability tests), divide it by a factor of safety of 1.5 to 10 depending on area drained and consequence of failure (C753 Table 25.2), keep the base at least 1 m above maximum groundwater, and ignore infiltration altogether where k is below about 1e-8 m/s; generic or mapped rates are acceptable only at outline stage.

### Cited Findings
- C753 Table 25.2 factors of safety (as extracted by Severn Trent): area drained <100 m2: 1.5 (no damage or inconvenience), 2 (minor damage to external areas or inconvenience, for example surface water on car parking), 10 (damage to buildings or structures, or major inconvenience, for example flooding of roads); 100-1000 m2: 1.5, 3, 10; >1000 m2: 1.5, 5, 10. "This factor is not required when using the BRE 365 method." [DESIGN, secondary quote of C753, read in primary text, p.19] — [Severn Trent, SuDS General Design Manual](https://www.stwater.co.uk/content/dam/stw/stw_buildinganddeveloping/suds/general-guidance/suds-general-design-manual.pdf)
- "In general, if infiltration rates are measured to be greater than 1 x 10-6 m/s then infiltration should be considered viable"; test at the design invert depth, using BRE Digest 365 soakaway testing and/or variable head permeability tests (BS 5930, BS EN ISO 22282). Checklist: rate > 1e-6 m/s; groundwater > 1 m below invert; no contamination; more than 5 m from property or structure. [RULE, water company, read in primary text, s.2.10 p.16-18] — [Severn Trent, SuDS General Design Manual](https://www.stwater.co.uk/content/dam/stw/stw_buildinganddeveloping/suds/general-guidance/suds-general-design-manual.pdf)
- C753 requires the base of an infiltration component to be at least 1 m above the maximum anticipated groundwater level. [DESIGN, quoted by Severn Trent s.2.10.2; consistent with the C753 infiltration assessment checklist question on ">1 m below the base"] — [Severn Trent manual](https://www.stwater.co.uk/content/dam/stw/stw_buildinganddeveloping/suds/general-guidance/suds-general-design-manual.pdf); [C753 infiltration assessment checklist (Nottinghamshire copy)](https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/media/2902386/appendix-b-c753-the-suds-manual-infiltration-assessment-checklist.pdf)
- C753 Table 20.1: infiltration is counted in storage design only for subgrade k of 1e-8 m/s or higher; below that "the infiltration of water is not allowed for in the storage design". [DESIGN, read in primary text] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- For the pavement surface itself, C753 applies a factor of safety of 10 (2500 mm/h new to 250 mm/h long term). [DESIGN, read in primary text] — [CIRIA C753 ch.20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)
- Essex LLFA guidance: only values from site infiltration testing should be used in calculations; generic rates from public soil and geology data are acceptable for outline applications if sources are stated; safety factor "most likely to be 2 for individual property soakaways" and Table 25.2 values for features draining larger areas; 10 % urban creep allowance. [RULE, LLFA, read in primary text] — [Essex Design Guide, drainage calculations guide](https://www.essexdesignguide.co.uk/suds/further-guidance/drainage-calculations-guide/)
- A susdrain/EPG briefing on soakaway testing notes that trapped air may reduce field hydraulic conductivity "by as much as a factor of two". [Technical briefing, search snippet only] — [susdrain EPG briefing: soakaway testing](https://www.susdrain.org/files/resources/briefings/EPG_Briefing_SoakwayTesting_V2.pdf)
- Southwark SFRA drainage hierarchy includes "use infiltration techniques, such as porous surfaces in non-clay areas". [Policy, read in extracted text, s.5.3] — [LB Southwark SFRA](https://www.southwark.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2024-05/southwark_council_sfra.pdf)

### Inferences
- [DERIVED] For a street-scale feature where failure floods a road (the Blackfriars Road case), C753 points to a factor of safety of 10. A measured 1e-6 m/s (3.6 mm/h) becomes a design 0.36 mm/h, about 0.25 mm in a 40-minute storm. Even a good measured 1e-5 m/s (36 mm/h) becomes 3.6 mm/h, about 2.4 mm in 40 minutes per m2 of infiltrating base. So under UK design rules, infiltration to ground is negligible within a cloudburst for any feature on this site; it matters for emptying the storage afterwards.
- This supports modelling all four intervention types as storage (surface ponding plus sub-surface voids) with a slow drain-down term, and presenting ground infiltration as a sensitivity case rather than a base assumption.
- Where sources differ: Severn Trent uses 1e-6 m/s as the viability threshold, while C753 Table 20.1 allows infiltration to be counted down to 1e-8 m/s for partial systems. The stricter figure is a water-company adoption rule; the C753 figure is the national design guide.

### Gaps
- BRE Digest 365 itself (paywalled) was not read. Its design storm (commonly cited as a 10-year event) and half-empty time (commonly cited as 24 hours) are from general knowledge and were not verified in this session.
- C753 Table 25.1 (typical infiltration coefficients by soil texture) could not be opened: the East Suffolk copy returned 404 and the West Berkshire copy 403. From recollection, unverified, it gives clay as below 3e-8 m/s and gravel as roughly 3e-4 to 3e-2 m/s; confirm against C753 p.546 before quoting.
- Thames Water and GLA sector guidance on design infiltration rates were not retrieved.
