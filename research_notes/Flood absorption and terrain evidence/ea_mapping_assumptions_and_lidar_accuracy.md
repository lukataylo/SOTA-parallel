## 1. The 2013 national mapping (uFMfSW / RoFSW): drainage, runoff, buildings, roads, grid, roughness, storms, thresholds

### Takeaway
The 2013 national model (JFlow+, 2 m grid) reduced urban rainfall to 70% and then subtracted 12 mm/h for drainage, raised building footprints by 0.3 m (floodable upstands, not walls), lowered roads by 0.125 m, used Manning's n of about 0.02 on tarmac (0.03 grass, 0.1 woodland), and ran 1, 3 and 6 hour storms at 1 in 30, 1 in 100 and 1 in 1000. The team's 12 mm/h matches the 2013 default exactly, but its n = 0.015 is lower than the national road value, and "buildings as walls" matches the superseded 2010 method, not the 2013 one.

### Cited Findings

Drainage allowance
- "In urban areas, rainfall is reduced to 70% to represent infiltration, then a rainfall reduction of 12mm/hr is applied to represent the effects of the drainage system." (section 4.2, printed p.13) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- The 12 mm/h figure is a national "typical" value from earlier studies that assumed a drainage service level between 1 in 5 and 1 in 30 years (centred on 1 in 10), a critical duration of 0.5 to 2 hours and 30% to 80% impermeable area. "The calculated range of sewer capacities was in the range between 5mm/hr and 54mm/hr; with a typical drainage removal rate of 12mm/hr across catchments in England and Wales." (printed p.13) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Local variants: "In areas of known low or high drainage capacity, LLFAs could substitute alternative values of 6mm/hr or 20mm/hr." (printed p.13) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf); contradicted on the upper value by SC120020 (Appendix D description), which says the rate "was changed to 6mm/hr in places identified as having a low drainage rate and 18mm/hr in areas identified as having high drainage rates" — [SC120020](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/603660e18fa8f5480ff52477/Improving_surface_water_flood_mapping_-_estimating_local_drainage_rates_-_report.pdf)
- "Drainage capacity is the source of greatest uncertainty in the modelling." (annex summary table, PDF p.39) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- SC120020 contains a worked local estimate of drainage system capacity of 18 mm/hour for Market Harborough, i.e. above the 12 mm/h default — [SC120020](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/603660e18fa8f5480ff52477/Improving_surface_water_flood_mapping_-_estimating_local_drainage_rates_-_report.pdf)

Runoff coefficients
- Urban: 70% runoff coefficient, "a good average runoff coefficient for built-up areas including gardens and green verges and a mix of city centre and more suburban land uses. The FEH catchment descriptor assumes a 70% coefficient for urban areas." (printed p.13) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Rural (2013): no fixed percentage; the ReFH losses model with NSRI "SERIES Hydrology" soil data, so percentage runoff rises with soil moisture through the storm. The same ReFH losses model was applied to "the green portions of urban areas" (printed p.13) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Earlier generations (comparison table, printed p.19): FMfSW 2010 used urban 70% and rural 39% with 12 mm/h; AStSWF 2008 used 100% runoff and 0 mm/h — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

Buildings
- "Ordnance Survey MasterMap data was used to raise the ground level within building footprints (according to the bare earth DTM) by an average of 0.3m. Typically the building is raised by 0.3m above the average height of the building footprint to give an upstand with a horizontal 'floor level', which deflects flow." (section 4.3.4, printed p.15) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- "Buildings are also represented so water can flow through them once the depth exceeds the height of the upstand. This is consistent with reports of flooding in steeper catchments where water is observed to pass through, rather than around, buildings." (section 4.3.4) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- The comparison table (printed p.19) describes the 2013 method as a "(typically) 0.3m high 'up-stand' and depth-varying roughness coefficients within the OS MasterMap building footprint", whereas FMfSW 2010 represented buildings "explicitly as unfloodable objects in the DTM. Building footprints raised by 5m" — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

Roads and kerbs
- "Road surfaces, selected from OS MasterMap data, were lowered by 0.125m (the height of a British Standard kerb) to better delineate these important pathways". "Other features such as fences, walls, dropped kerbs and speed bumps may not be explicitly represented within the DTM." (section 4.3.6, printed p.16) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

Grid, DTM and model
- "Flood modelling was carried out on a 2m resolution grid for all England and Wales." Source DTM varied from 0.25 m to 5 m and was resampled to 2 m (section 4.3.3). The DTM was the "FMfSW DTM 2012" LIDAR/NEXTMap composite: Environment Agency LIDAR 67.7%, Infoterra LIDAR 1.0%, NEXTMap SAR 31.3% (section 4.3.1) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Software: JFlow+ 2D model solving the shallow water equations, direct rainfall method, about 7,100 tiles of 5 km x 5 km with a 500 m overlap; simulation ends at rainfall duration + 3 hours (sections 4.4.1, 4.4.2 and comparison table) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Bridges and viaducts: "Some flow paths under bridges and through embankments are not captured within the DTM because the top of the feature is represented rather than the opening through it." The list includes road and railway embankments, bridges, subways and tunnels. "Approximately 91,000 edits were made to the DTM" to open flow paths through these "flyover" features (sections 4.3.5, printed pp.15-16) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

Manning's n
- "The Manning's n values are varied according to Ordnance Survey MasterMap Topography Layer Feature Code... Typical values used were 0.02 for tarmac roads or pavement, 0.03 for grassland areas and 0.1 for heavy woodland and vegetation." (section 4.4.3, printed p.17) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- FMfSW 2010 used n = 0.1 rural and 0.03 urban; AStSWF 2008 used 0.1 everywhere (comparison table, printed p.20) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

Storms and probabilities
- "The modelling includes storm durations of 1, 3 and 6 hours for each" probability; a short storm of around 1 hour "generally better represents" steep and urban response (section 4.2.1, printed p.12). Rainfall depths from FEH depth-duration-frequency curves; "50% summer rainfall profile" (annex summary table, PDF p.39) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Annual probabilities: 3.3% (1 in 30), 1% (1 in 100), 0.1% (1 in 1000) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

Hazard and depth thresholds
- "Hazard rating = depth x (velocity + 0.5) + debris factor", debris factor 0.5 for depths up to 0.25 m and 1.0 above. Classes: low 0.5-0.75, moderate 0.75-1.25, significant 1.25-2.0, extreme 2.0 and above (section 7.5, printed p.27) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Output filter: "removed flooded areas with a very low hazard rating below 0.575"; "The RoFSW is filtered by hazard rating, rather than a depth threshold" (sections 6.2, 7.5 and Annex E.2) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Depth bands (m): below 0.15, 0.15-0.30, 0.30-0.60, 0.60-0.90, 0.90-1.20, above 1.20; the public map grouped them as below 300 mm, 300-900 mm, above 900 mm (section 7.3, printed p.26) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

Validation
- In Cheltenham (summer 2007 floods) the majority of flooded properties were correctly identified; in Rhondda Cynon Taff "there is little correlation between the national flood map and the locations of flood incidents". "Models of this type tend to perform better in steeper rural areas than in flat urban areas." (section 9) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Constant 12 mm/h drainage loss: matches the 2013 default.** It was a national average, with a stated plausible range of 5 to 54 mm/h, and the report itself calls drainage capacity the greatest source of uncertainty. The team should treat 12 mm/h as a central value and test 6 and 18 to 20 mm/h (the LLFA variants). Whether Southwark was modelled at 12 or at a local variant in 2013 was not established.
- **The 12 mm/h was applied after a 70% reduction, not on its own.** Net rainfall in 2013 urban cells was 0.7 x intensity minus 12 mm/h. If the team subtracts 12 mm/h from 100% of rainfall, it generates more runoff than the 2013 national model at every intensity (for example at 40 mm/h: 28 mm/h net versus 16 mm/h).
- **Roofs draining to sewers: not how the national method works, and no source supports it as the national approach.** The 2013 model applied the same urban rule (70%, minus 12 mm/h) to every urban cell by direct rainfall, including building footprints; roofs were not given a separate route to the sewer. Sending all roof runoff to sewers on top of a 12 mm/h loss would count drainage capacity twice relative to the national method, because the 12 mm/h figure already stands for "property drainage, highway drainage and public sewers" combined (that wording is from a search snippet of the SEPA summary of the same method and was not opened; treat it as unverified).
- **Buildings as walls: does not match 2013 (or 2025).** The 2013 and 2025 methods both use a 0.3 m floodable upstand. Solid, unfloodable buildings (raised 5 m) were the 2010 FMfSW method, which the 2013 pilot studies moved away from. Walls will give more deflection and deeper ponding against buildings than the national maps.
- **Manning's n = 0.015 everywhere: lower than the national road value of 0.02, and the national method varies n by land cover.** For a dense Southwark square that is mostly paved the practical difference is 0.015 versus 0.02 on hard surfaces, plus the missing 0.03 for any grass and the missing depth-varying roughness inside buildings. Lower n gives faster, shallower flow on slopes.
- **Kerbs:** the national model burned roads in by 0.125 m because a 2 m grid cannot resolve kerbs. A 1 m DTM also does not reliably resolve a 0.125 m kerb, so the team should decide explicitly whether to lower road cells.
- The 0.575 hazard filter is equivalent to a depth of 0.15 m in still water (0.15 x 0.5 + 0.5 = 0.575), and less depth where water is moving. This is arithmetic from the formula, not a statement in the report.

### Gaps
- The full MasterMap feature-code to Manning's n lookup table and the depth-varying roughness values used inside building footprints were not found; RoFSW-2019 gives only the three "typical" values. They are probably in the JBA/Halcrow technical methodology reports, which were not located online.
- The discrepancy between 20 mm/h (RoFSW-2019) and 18 mm/h (SC120020) for the high-drainage variant was not resolved.
- Which drainage rate was applied in Southwark in 2013 was not found.
- The SC120020 report was only searched for drainage figures, not read in full; its findings on real local drainage rates beyond the Market Harborough example are not summarised here.

## 2. What changed in NaFRA2 (the new national model, published January 2025)

### Takeaway
The 2025 surface water map still uses a 2 m grid, JFlow and a 30 cm building uplift, but the loss model changed: urban ("manmade") cells are 100% impervious with a default drainage loss of 18 mm/h (13 to 25 mm/h with local information), storm durations are 1, 6 and 18 hours, and flooding shallower than 75 mm is removed. The team's 12 mm/h is the old default, not the current one.

### Cited Findings
- "In urban areas (identified using Ordnance Survey data), the surface is represented as 100% impervious. However, we do make allowances for subsurface drainage systems." (section 3.2, Rainfall and losses) — [RoFSW-2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map)
- "We apply a drainage rate in millimetres per hour (mm/hr) as an ongoing loss within all model cells classed as manmade. The default loss rate is 18 mm/hr. This varies between 13 and 25 mm/hr if local information is available or for calibration/validation purposes." (section 3.2) — [RoFSW-2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map)
- Non-urban areas are modelled using soil moisture and other factors; rainfall is mapped on a 1 km grid — [RoFSW-2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map)
- Terrain: "Our Integrated Height Model (IHM) 2019 dataset provides the basis for the Digital Terrain Model (DTM) used in the hydraulic modelling. This assigns a ground level to every 2m x 2m model cell." — [RoFSW-2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map)
- Buildings: "Building footprints are raised above their surroundings as part of the modelling process. We have used an uplift of 30cm above the 'zero' elevation of the building. This means that in the raw model outputs, buildings can look like they are not at risk of flooding." Footprints are then infilled in the mapped output depending on how much of the exterior wall is wet — [RoFSW-2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map)
- Minimum depth: "we removed flooding to a depth of less than 75mm. This is because in surface water flood modelling, most land is covered by some water as rain falls across the area... it is generally unlikely to prevent access or cause flooding to properties." Dry islands under 50 m2 are infilled and wet areas under 100 m2 removed — [RoFSW-2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map)
- Software: "The primary modelling software used in the New National Model is JFLOW, a 2D hydraulic model with limited 1D elements." (Q45) — [NaFRA2-QA](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)
- Drainage: accounted for "explicitly via the inclusion of culverts... and implicitly through the use of a 'drainage layer' that is used to approximate the capacity of local drainage networks to absorb runoff in an event before becoming overwhelmed." (Q43). LLFAs were invited to report "areas with particularly effective or particularly poor drainage" (Q52) — [NaFRA2-QA](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)
- Rainfall: "Rainfall depths used in modelling surface water flooding in the New National Model are from the FEH13 DDF model, and 3 rainfall durations have been modelled: 1 hour, 6 hour and 18 hours." (Q49) — [NaFRA2-QA](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)
- Scenarios: present-day 1 in 5, 1 in 20, 1 in 30, 1 in 100, 1 in 200 and 1 in 1,000; surface water climate change runs at +20% and +60% rainfall for the 1 in 1,000; an undrained 1 in 1,000 run was also made (Q44) — [NaFRA2-QA](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)
- Published surface water depth bands on Flood Map for Planning: below 150 mm, 150-300, 300-600, 600-900, 900-1,200, 1,200-2,300, above 2,300 mm, for the 3.3%, 1% and 0.1% events (Q98) — [NaFRA2-QA](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)
- The national product combines the new national model with "more than 200 detailed local models of surface water" — [NaFRA2-QA](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)
- Result: about 4.6 million properties in areas at risk from surface water, "a 43% increase on our previous assessment"; "1 in 8 (13%) of properties in London are in areas at high or medium risk of surface water flooding. This is twice the rate compared to the national total" — [NaFRA-2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024)

### Inferences
- **Drainage: the current national default is 18 mm/h from 100% runoff, not 12 mm/h from 70%.** The two schemes give the same net rainfall at an intensity of 20 mm/h (0.7 x 20 - 12 = 2; 20 - 18 = 2). Above 20 mm/h the 2025 scheme produces more runoff than 2013; below it, less. For design storms that matter in London (1 hour, 1 in 30 and rarer, well above 20 mm/h) the 2025 scheme is the more severe.
- If the team uses 100% runoff minus 12 mm/h, it is 6 mm/h more pessimistic than the 2025 national default and at the edge of (just below) the 13 to 25 mm/h range the Environment Agency allows. That is defensible as a cautious choice for an area with poor drainage, but it should be described as such, not as "the national method".
- Resolution did not change for surface water: both generations are 2 m. The "50 m to 2 m" change often quoted refers to the rivers and sea product. The team's 1 m grid is finer than either national model.
- Building representation is essentially unchanged (0.3 m uplift) between 2013 and 2025.
- The switch of durations from 1, 3, 6 hours to 1, 6, 18 hours matters little for a 512 m urban square, where the 1 hour storm is the relevant one in both schemes.

### Gaps
- The detailed NaFRA2 methods documents ("Overview of NaFRA2 methods for national flood risk products", "New National Modelling Project Report and Product Definition Document") are on an Environment Agency SharePoint site for risk management authorities and are not public. Manning's n values, road lowering, treatment of bridges and viaducts, and the percentage-runoff method for green space in the 2025 model could not be confirmed.
- RoFSW-2025 does not say whether roads are still lowered by 0.125 m or what roughness values are used.
- Whether Southwark's published surface water map comes from the national model or from a local (LLFA) model was not checked.
- The NaFRA2-QA document was read from a council-hosted copy; a search snippet gave its version as 12.0 dated 13 May 2026, which was not verified in the document itself.

## 3. Stated accuracy of Environment Agency LIDAR and how the DTM is filtered

### Takeaway
The official figures are consistent once absolute and relative error are separated: the specification is absolute vertical error below ±15 cm RMSE (the figure on the 2022 composite metadata), recent surveys achieve about ±5 cm, and relative (random) error is expected to be no more than ±5 cm; horizontal absolute error is ±40 cm. All of these are measured on flat hard surfaces such as car parks, so they are a best case and say nothing about error next to buildings or under vegetation.

### Cited Findings
- 2022 composite DTM metadata: "All individual LIDAR surveys going into the production of the composite had a vertical accuracy of +/-15cm RMSE." — [DTM-1m](https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/01b3ee39-da3f-47b6-83da-dc98e73a461f)
- Technical note: "Our specifications require the absolute height error to be less than ±15cm. This is the root mean squared error or RMSE. It quantifies the error or difference between the Ground Truth Survey and the LIDAR data. With our more recent surveys we see this fall to about ±5cm. We expect the relative height error (random error) to be no more than ±5cm." — [LIDAR-FAQ](https://www.owenboswarva.com/opendata/EA/Environment_Agency_LIDAR_Open_Data_FAQ_v5.pdf)
- Horizontal: "The absolute spatial error in our LIDAR data is ±40cm. For our datasets at 2m, 1m and 50cm resolution, this error is effectively absorbed in the pixels of the raster image." Relative horizontal accuracy is 1/5500 x flying height, i.e. 0.18 m at 1,000 m above ground — [LIDAR-FAQ](https://www.owenboswarva.com/opendata/EA/Environment_Agency_LIDAR_Open_Data_FAQ_v5.pdf)
- Quality control threshold: "All LIDAR surveys must report an error of less than +/-15cm RMSE and 10cm for standard deviation and random error to pass quality control." — [Ground-Truth](https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/123fd228-9033-4cac-afc4-4084977dd30a)
- How accuracy is measured: "a ground truth survey is a collection of a few hundred points captured on a flat, unambiguous surface such as a tarmac car park or tennis court using GPS. Each ground truth point has an accuracy of +/-3cm R.M.S.E"; a ground truth survey may be reused for LIDAR surveys for up to 5 years (3 for coastal) — [Ground-Truth](https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/123fd228-9033-4cac-afc4-4084977dd30a)
- The flood mapping report repeats both figures: "All LIDAR within the composite DTM has a quoted accuracy of ±15 cm or better in its original form, although it is thought that the accuracy of Environment Agency LIDAR data has been closer to ±5cm since 2005." (section 4.3.2) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- DTM filtering: "The DTM (Digital Terrain Model) is produced from the last or only laser pulse returned to the sensor. We remove surface objects from the Digital Surface Model (DSM), using bespoke algorithms and manual editing of the data, to produce a terrain model of just the surface." Coverage about 99% of England at 1 m; heights in metres above Ordnance Datum Newlyn via OSTN15 — [DTM-1m](https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/01b3ee39-da3f-47b6-83da-dc98e73a461f)
- National LIDAR Programme DTM: "created from the last return LIDAR pulse classified as ground, filtering out surface objects. Manual filtering is undertaken on the DTM to improve the automated classification routines to produce a most likely ground surface model. Areas of no data, such as water bodies, are also filled to ensure there are no gaps in the model." Points are classified automatically into ground and low, medium and high vegetation. 302 survey blocks flown in winter (about November to April) between January 2017 and February 2023 — [NLP](https://data.gov.uk/api/2/rest/harvestobject/bb686612-bf72-485a-a888-d7002798195c/html)
- Known filtering failure: classification "is a fully automatic process... An example of this might be where a large flat rooftop is classified as 'ground'... When producing a DTM surface from these ground points, the building object will remain present within this surface. Manual editing of the raster surface is then carried out... to remove or retain object(s) incorrectly classified" — [LIDAR-FAQ](https://www.owenboswarva.com/opendata/EA/Environment_Agency_LIDAR_Open_Data_FAQ_v5.pdf)
- Composite construction: surveys captured between 6 June 2000 and 2 April 2022, "resampled and merged as a composite product to give the newest, best possible coverage"; where finer data was resampled, "a bilinear interpolation was used before being merged". Each tile ships with a GeoPackage listing the survey ID, resolution and flight dates of every survey used (Executive summary and metadata schema) — [Composite-Guide](https://environment.data.gov.uk/api/file/download?fileDataSetId=b9a415e5-452d-4bbd-90ac-e2568cd2dbc5&fileName=LIDAR%20Composite%20Technical%20Information%20Guide.docx)
- Survey joins: overlapping surveys are merged with a feathered buffer: 30 m for 2 m data, 25 m for 1 m data, 20 m for 50 cm, 15 m for 25 cm — [LIDAR-FAQ](https://www.owenboswarva.com/opendata/EA/Environment_Agency_LIDAR_Open_Data_FAQ_v5.pdf)
- Bridges and viaducts in Environment Agency DTMs as used for flood mapping: "Some flow paths under bridges and through embankments are not captured within the DTM because the top of the feature is represented rather than the opening through it" (road and railway embankments, bridges, subways, tunnels); the national modellers hand-edited about 91,000 such locations (section 4.3.5) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The two accuracy figures do not conflict.** ±15 cm RMSE is the contractual pass/fail limit on absolute error (LIDAR versus independent GPS on Ordnance Datum), and it is what the metadata quotes. ±5 cm is (a) what recent surveys actually achieve in absolute terms and (b) the expected relative, point-to-point random error. A further QC limit of 10 cm applies to the standard deviation and random error.
- **The team's 5 cm standard deviation matches the Environment Agency's stated relative (random) error and the "recent surveys" absolute figure, but only for open hard ground.** It is the right order for roads and paved areas, and optimistic as a blanket figure: the QC limit that surveys are actually held to is 10 cm standard deviation, and the validation points are deliberately placed on flat tarmac, away from building edges, vegetation and filtered areas.
- Absolute (datum) error is close to a constant offset across a single survey and a 512 m square will usually sit inside one or two surveys. A constant offset does not change flow paths or ponding depths, so the relevant quantity for the flood model is the relative error plus the interpolation error under removed objects, not the 15 cm absolute figure.
- Under every building footprint the DTM is interpolated, not measured. In the team's model this matters less if buildings are walls or upstands, but it does affect the ground level assigned at building edges and therefore door-threshold depths.
- The ±40 cm horizontal error is under half a 1 m cell, so features can be displaced by up to about one cell; this is one physical reason to expect error correlation over at least a cell or two.
- For Southwark specifically (railway viaducts into London Bridge, many arches and underpasses): the national method's own experience is that such openings are missing from the bare-earth DTM and must be edited by hand. The team should inspect the 2022 DTM at each viaduct and underpass in the square and check the tile's GeoPackage for survey dates and any survey join crossing the square.

### Gaps
- No Environment Agency document was found that states how bridges and viaducts are treated in the 2022 LIDAR Composite DTM specifically (removed to ground level, left in, or interpolated). The Composite-Guide is silent on it; the only explicit statement is the RoFSW-2019 one about the 2012 flood-mapping DTM.
- The official gov.uk copy of the LIDAR technical note / FAQ v5 (March 2016) was not found; the figures come from a third-party mirror of the Environment Agency document. The wording agrees with the official RoFSW-2019 and Ground-Truth pages, but the note predates the National LIDAR Programme.
- The "LIDAR Ground Truth Surveys Technical Note.docx" linked from the Ground-Truth record was not opened.
- No published Environment Agency figure was found for DTM accuracy in filtered areas (under vegetation, next to buildings) or for the actual ground truth results of the survey(s) covering Southwark; the Ground-Truth record says these are available on request.
- The National LIDAR Programme metadata record itself gives no numeric accuracy figure; a separate programme specification was not found.

## 4. Published evidence on the spatial structure of LiDAR DEM error

### Takeaway
I found one fully verified geostatistical study of 1 m LiDAR DEM error used in an inundation Monte Carlo (Leon et al. 2014): error standard deviation 0.18 m, correlation range 102 m (65 m after removing land-cover and terrain effects), smallest errors on roads, houses and bare earth. No source was found that supports a 3 m correlation length, and no study specific to Environment Agency LIDAR error structure was found; the team's 3 m figure is unsupported and much shorter than the published value.

### Cited Findings
- Leon et al. used a 1 m LiDAR DEM with stated vertical RMSE within 0.15 m and horizontal RMSE within 0.45 m (similar to the Environment Agency specification), checked against 407 RTK GPS points. Measured elevation error: mean 0.04 m, standard deviation 0.18 m, range -0.49 to 0.48 m — [Leon-2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4177217/)
- Variogram (Matern model): "The spatial structure of elevation errors showed a range of 102 m and partial sill of 0.023." For residuals after regression on land cover and terrain: "Both the range and partial sill of the variogram fitted to GLS-derived residuals were smaller (65 and 0.018, respectively)." — [Leon-2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4177217/)
- Error by surface: errors were smallest on houses, roads and bare earth and largest on vegetated areas (sparse trees, mangrove) and sandy beaches; land cover, NDVI, slope and convexity together explained 14% of error variance — [Leon-2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4177217/)
- Method and effect: 1,000 sequential Gaussian simulations of the error field added to the DEM; at the 1% exceedance probability the inundated area was "approximately 11% larger than that mapped using the deterministic approach", while the median was about 0.5% smaller — [Leon-2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4177217/)
- Hawker, Bates, Neal and Rougier (2018, Frontiers in Earth Science) argue that RMSE-type statistics treat DEM error as aspatial, that errors are spatially correlated, and that flood studies should simulate ensembles of plausible DEMs rather than use one (from search-result excerpts; full text not opened) — [Hawker et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2018.00233/full)
- Fewtrell et al. (2011) found that road camber and kerbs resolved by terrestrial LiDAR significantly affect simulated urban surface water flows, and that errors from coarse topography exceed differences between numerical schemes (abstract-level, via the publisher summary) — [Fewtrell et al. 2011](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/benchmarking-urban-flood-models-of-varying-complexity-and-scale-u/)
- Ozdemir et al. (2013, HESS) state that airborne LiDAR terrain models of about 50 cm to 3 m resolution are typical for urban flood models and that small features such as road cambers and kerbs cannot be distinguished in airborne LiDAR data (search-result excerpt; full text not opened) — [Ozdemir et al. 2013](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/17/4015/2013/hess-17-4015-2013.pdf)
- The Environment Agency's own validation samples only "a flat, unambiguous surface such as a tarmac car park or tennis court", so its stated accuracy is an open hard surface figure by construction — [Ground-Truth](https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/123fd228-9033-4cac-afc4-4084977dd30a)
- The Environment Agency notes that large flat roofs can be misclassified as ground and remain in the DTM until manually edited, i.e. gross errors near buildings are a recognised failure mode — [LIDAR-FAQ](https://www.owenboswarva.com/opendata/EA/Environment_Agency_LIDAR_Open_Data_FAQ_v5.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Terrain error of 5 cm standard deviation correlated over 3 m: the magnitude is defensible for hard surfaces; the 3 m correlation length is not supported by anything found.** The one verified estimate for comparable 1 m LiDAR is 65 to 102 m, with a standard deviation (0.18 m) more than three times the team's. That site is a vegetated coastal area in Australia, so its magnitude overstates error on London tarmac, but its qualitative result (error correlated over tens of metres, smallest on roads and roofs) is the best evidence available.
- Correlation length matters for the result. Short-range (3 m) noise at 5 cm mostly creates and removes small pits and has little effect on where water collects; long-range error tilts whole streets and can move ponding between neighbouring low points. A 3 m length therefore probably understates the flood-extent uncertainty the Monte Carlo is meant to capture. A reasonable design is to run at least two lengths (for example about 3 m for sensor and interpolation noise and several tens of metres for strip and filtering error) and report sensitivity.
- A two-class error model is better justified than a uniform one: about 5 cm on open paved ground (Environment Agency relative error), and a larger value (the 10 cm QC limit is a sourced candidate) within a few metres of buildings and under trees, where the DTM is interpolated. The specific width of that zone is a modelling choice; no source gives it.
- The literature the Environment Agency itself cites for the national method implies that unresolved kerbs and cambers (0.1 to 0.125 m) are an error of the same size as or larger than the 5 cm random error being simulated.

### Gaps
- No study quantifying the variogram or correlation length of Environment Agency LIDAR error was found.
- No verified source was found giving LiDAR DEM error statistics separately for open hard surfaces versus the zone adjacent to buildings in an urban area. Leon-2014 gives per-class results only as a box plot.
- Work by Hunter, Neal, Savage, Wechsler, Oksanen and Sarjakoski, and Abily named in the brief was searched for but not retrieved in full, so no figures from it are reported. These remain leads to check: Wechsler and Kroll (2006) and Oksanen and Sarjakoski (2005, 2006) on correlated DEM error simulation; Abily et al. (2016) on high-resolution urban DEM uncertainty; Hunter et al. (2008) urban benchmark. Anything attributed to them from memory would be unverified.
- "Typical values used in Monte Carlo DEM-error studies" could not be tabulated from verified sources beyond Leon-2014; one search excerpt mentioned other variogram ranges (about 25 m, and 50 m and 180 m in a landslide study) but for different data and these were not checked.

## 5. What depth counts as property flooding in UK practice

### Takeaway
The Environment Agency's own stated thresholds are: 0.15 m "cause property flooding in some areas", 0.30 m "likely to cause property flooding... based on average property threshold levels", floor levels "often by around 20cm" above outside ground, and flooding under 75 mm removed as unlikely to affect property. A 0.2 m to 0.3 m depth at the building edge is the best-sourced definition of likely internal flooding, with 0.15 m as the lower bound.

### Cited Findings
- 2013 depth-band rationale (section 7.3): "At 0.15m, flooding would: typically exceed kerb height (standard kerb height is 125mm); likely exceed the level of a damp-proof course; cause property flooding in some areas." — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- "At 0.30m flooding is likely to cause property flooding. This is based on average property threshold levels." (section 7.3) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- "Property-level flood resilience measures are typically effective up to a water depth of 0.60m above floor level... the maximum flood depth where resilience measures are still effective may be in a range between 0.60m and 0.90m above ground level." (section 7.3) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- Building method: the 2013 model "allows flood water to flow into the building footprint once the water depth exceeds 300mm" (annex summary table) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- "RoFSW does not take individual property threshold heights into account so the map shows areas that may potentially flood but cannot accurately predict the impacts on individual properties." (section 8) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)
- 2024 national assessment: "Our assessment of flood depths is based on outside ground levels. It is important to note that property floor levels are generally higher than the surrounding land – often by around 20cm." — [NaFRA-2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024)
- The 2024 assessment uses 30 cm as its headline depth: "184,200 properties in areas at high risk are likely to flood to depths of 30cm or higher from surface water. This is 1 in 6 (17%) of all properties in areas at high risk"; "83% percent of properties at high risk are in areas with likely flood depths of less than 30cm" — [NaFRA-2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024)
- 2025 map minimum: flooding "to a depth of less than 75mm" is removed because it is "generally unlikely to prevent access or cause flooding to properties"; the public depth maps show the chance of exceeding stated depths, with 20 cm given as the example — [RoFSW-2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map)
- 2025 depth bands start at below 150 mm, then 150-300 mm and 300-600 mm (Q98) — [NaFRA2-QA](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)
- Property counts in the 2013 validation "are sensitive to buffering and the thresholds that are applied" (section 9) — [RoFSW-2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible three-level scheme for the team, all traceable to Environment Agency text: below 0.075 m = not counted as flooding; 0.15 m = possible property flooding (kerb and damp-proof course exceeded); 0.20 m = typical floor level above ground; 0.30 m = likely internal flooding. Reporting counts at 0.15, 0.2 and 0.3 m shows the sensitivity the Environment Agency itself warns about.
- These thresholds are of the same size as the terrain uncertainty: a 5 to 10 cm DTM error, a 12.5 cm unresolved kerb and a 20 cm assumed threshold mean a single-property flooded/not-flooded call is not supportable; results should be given as probabilities or counts with ranges. The Environment Agency makes the same point about its own maps.
- National property counts ("in areas at risk") are based on the mapped extent touching the property, at outside ground level, not on water exceeding a door threshold, so they are not comparable with a threshold-exceedance count.

### Gaps
- No insurer or Flood Re depth threshold was found. No figure from the Multi-Coloured Manual (the standard damage-appraisal reference) on assumed threshold height was retrieved; searches returned nothing usable.
- No sourced figure was found for the Building Regulations door threshold or damp-proof course height (the commonly quoted 150 mm damp-proof course height is not sourced here).
- The exact rule NaFRA2 uses to decide that a property is "in an area at risk" (how much of the footprint or perimeter must be wet) is in the non-public methods documents.
- No London or Southwark-specific survey of real door threshold heights was found; older terraced and basement properties may sit at or below pavement level, which the 20 cm average would not capture.
