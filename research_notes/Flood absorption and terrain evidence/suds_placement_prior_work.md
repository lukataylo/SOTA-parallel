# Prior work on spatial placement of SuDS / green infrastructure for urban pluvial flooding

Reading note for the report writer. I ran 20 web searches and 12 page fetches. Only 4 fetches returned readable content (the CONFIGURE arXiv abstract, the Iliadis arXiv abstract, the Vercruysse repository record, and the full Dobkowitz et al. 2026 HESS article). Full-text PDFs could not be parsed in this environment and ScienceDirect, Exeter ORE, CORE, Springer and HAL were blocked. So, except for Dobkowitz et al. 2026, every finding below rests on an abstract or a search-result summary, not on the full paper. Where I could not confirm authors, numbers or which paper a search summary referred to, I say so in the bullet. Numbers of model runs, population sizes and grid-cell counts were mostly not recoverable and are listed under Gaps.

## 1. What optimisation methods are used for spatial placement, at what spatial unit, with how many runs?

### Takeaway
The dominant approach is an evolutionary algorithm (NSGA-II, less often NSGA-III, simulated annealing, PSO) wrapped around SWMM with sub-catchments as the decision unit; optimisation against a 2D or 1D-2D model is described in the literature itself as rare because of run time, and the recent responses are surrogates (neural networks, radial basis functions) and one NSGA-II-on-CityCAT framework. I found no gradient, adjoint or marginal-sensitivity method applied to SuDS placement on a 2D model.

### Cited Findings
- Rehman, Glenis, Lewis and Kilsby (2024), "Multi-objective optimisation framework for Blue-Green Infrastructure placement using detailed flood model" (CONFIGURE), Journal of Hydrology 638, 131571. NSGA-II coupled directly to the CityCAT 2D hydrodynamic model; decision variables are permeable-surface "intervention zones" of varying size and number; storms of 30-year and 100-year return period. Abstract-level findings: designs with smaller interventions in many zones "save a good fraction of investment" but need more compute to optimise; the optimal spatial configuration changes with storm severity; the framework finds optimal solutions "in a fraction of the time" needed to trial all options. No numerical results in the abstract. — [arXiv 2310.13989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13989)
- Xia and colleagues used a radial-basis-function surrogate optimiser (DYCORS) for LID placement on a coupled 1D-2D inundation model with 103 decision variables; it beat TuRBO and PSO at equal budget. The paper states that most previous LID optimisation used 1D models, which give no spatial flood information, and that optimisation on 2D or 1D-2D models "was rarely explored due to the expensive computing time". Authors beyond Xia, journal and year not confirmed (search summary only). — [Politecnico di Milano repository record](https://re.public.polimi.it/handle/11311/1287719)
- Tansar et al., critical review of optimisation for green-grey stormwater infrastructure: recommends surrogate-based optimisation to overcome the computational burden of conventional simulation-optimisation. Journal and year not confirmed. — [PolyU repository PDF](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/109204/1/jws0731135.pdf)
- A 2023 Water paper on sponge-city detention volume used a neural-network surrogate with NSGA-II and reports a 90.03% runtime saving with Pareto quality preserved. — [Water 15(15), 2705](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/15/2705)
- Feng, Yang and Li (2023), Water Resources Management: multilayer-perceptron surrogate for LID placement area; optimisation cost reduced to 0.37% of conventional SWMM-based numerical optimisation. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-023-03630-w)
- Typical SWMM plus NSGA-II studies optimise LID area per sub-catchment or per site against cost, runoff reduction and node overload: a 2025 PLOS ONE study of old Beijing residential communities and a 2025 Land study of a campus. — [PLOS ONE via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12074530); [Land 14(7), 1434](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-445X/14/7/1434)
- A SWMM plus NSGA-III framework optimises quantity, placement and connectivity of grey-green-blue infrastructure for the Upper Ting River watershed, China. — [Journal of Hydrology: Regional Studies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214581824005056)
- A two-stage stochastic programme places green infrastructure to minimise expected runoff under precipitation uncertainty; it does not resolve individual parcels. — [Omega](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305048318314312)
- A "Marginal-Cost-based Greedy Strategy" for green infrastructure placement exists and is described in a review as computationally intensive. Original paper not identified. — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10943341)
- Reinforcement learning: the only hit was hierarchical RL for mobile pump scheduling and placement trained on multi-GPU hydrodynamic simulations (Loughborough), not SuDS. — [Loughborough repository](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/A_coupled_human_and_natural_systems_CHANS_framework_integrated_with_reinforcement_learning_for_urban_flood_mitigation/27961617)
- A 2026 arXiv preprint ("Optimising Urban Flood Resilience") states that SWMM is the predominant model for which optimisation tools are being developed. — [arXiv 2604.18620](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18620)

### Inferences
- The standard spatial unit is the SWMM sub-catchment (hectares), with tens to roughly a hundred decision variables. The finest unit I found on a 2D model is the CONFIGURE "permeable zone", which is a polygon of existing surface, not a grid cell.
- The field treats the hydrodynamic model as a black-box evaluator. A method that ranks locations from terrain structure before any solver run would sit outside the mainstream simulation-optimisation line, but see section 3 for the screening tools that already do part of this.

### Gaps
- Number of model evaluations, population size and generations: not recovered for any study. Not stated in the CONFIGURE abstract; full texts unreadable here.
- CONFIGURE study area, grid resolution, zone sizes and cost-benefit numbers: not recovered. The team must read the full paper, because it is the nearest competitor on "2D solver in the loop".
- No dedicated review of RL for LID placement was found (one search). I cannot say it does not exist.

## 2. Do any studies place measures at grid-cell or few-square-metre scale in a 2D model, and what did they find on placement versus area?

### Takeaway
I found no study that places few-square-metre absorbing patches at individual 1 m grid cells in a 2D hydrodynamic model and compares location against size. The nearest work is the Newcastle CityCAT line (Vercruysse et al. 2019; Iliadis, Glenis and Kilsby; Rehman et al. 2024), which identifies flood "source" cells by intervention in the solver, and the equal-area placement comparisons in SWMM, whose results on upstream versus downstream conflict.

### Cited Findings
- Vercruysse, Dawson, Glenis, Bertsch, Wright and Kilsby (2019), "Developing spatial prioritization criteria for integrated urban flood management based on a source-to-impact flood analysis", Journal of Hydrology 578, 124038. A "spatial rainfall cell dependency analysis" with CityCAT in Newcastle upon Tyne: locations are ranked by their contribution to total flood extent, to maximum depths, to flooded roads and green spaces, and to likelihood of exposure. Conclusions: catchment-wide thinking is needed, and different criteria give different priority locations. Source-cell size and number of simulations are not in the abstract. — [White Rose repository record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/149868/)
- Iliadis, Glenis and Kilsby, "A cost-benefit 'source-receptor' framework for implementation of Blue-Green flood risk management" (arXiv 2023, v2 March 2024; submitted to Journal of Hydrology; a search result indicates it appeared in Hydrology Research, April 2024, which I did not confirm). CityCAT, Newcastle city centre, about 1 million cells, 60-minute storms from 1-in-10 to 1-in-100 years. Five steps: identify sources of surface flooding, identify receptor buildings, link them by cost-benefit, and test added blue-green infrastructure at source locations using available green space. — [arXiv 2311.00420](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00420); cell count and storms from the search summary of [the arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.00420)
- Iliadis' 2024 Newcastle PhD thesis covers the same framework and "optimal locations" for blue-green infrastructure. — [Newcastle theses](https://theses.ncl.ac.uk/jspui/handle/10443/6320)
- Rehman et al. (2024): many small permeable zones are more cost-effective than fewer large ones; optimal layout depends on return period. — [arXiv 2310.13989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13989)
- A Berlin study, "Providers of decentralized blue-green flood adaptation measures protect others, not themselves, even in flat urban terrain" (International Journal of Disaster Risk Reduction, 2025), combined 2D maximum depths with terrain flow directions and found upstream-downstream provider-beneficiary relations even in flat terrain. Authors and numbers not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S221242092500545X)
- Equal-total-area comparison in a drainage-network model: uniform placement gave the most flood mitigation; upstream, centre and downstream placements performed similarly to each other and worse; LID closer to flooded locations gave larger reductions. Authors and exact title not confirmed (Water Resources Management, 2021-22). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-021-03036-6)
- A Journal of Hydrology genetic-algorithm study found the optimiser favoured a higher share of LID in midstream sub-catchments than upstream or downstream, and observed a reversal of the peak-reduction effect once surface storage was exhausted. The search summary did not say which paper; likely "Investigating the effectiveness and optimal spatial arrangement of low-impact development facilities" (2019). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169419307280)
- A Taipei study (SWMM plus simulated annealing on benefit/cost) found upstream areas suit buffer-type LID and highland areas suit retaining LID. Likely "Optimization of low impact development layout designs for megacity flood mitigation" (Journal of Hydrology, 2018); mapping not confirmed. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169418305560)
- An EPA SWMM study (AGU 2021 abstract, "Strategic placement of green infrastructure in urban stormwater networks") concluded there is no single best placement and that placement near the outlet works best at low rainfall intensity. — [NASA ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021AGUFM.H33A..02C/abstract)
- A small-catchment case study found green infrastructure hazard benefits propagate downstream only for storms of about three hours or less. — [Advances in Water Resources](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S030917081630481X)
- A 2021 Hydrology paper optimised green-grey infrastructure with a 2D shallow-water model with Green-Ampt infiltration and per-cell damage from maximum depth; infiltration lowered but did not eliminate flood depths. — [Hydrology 8(3), 110](https://doi.org/10.3390/hydrology8030110)
- Webber's Exeter PhD thesis (CADDIES-based rapid scenario screening) reports that intervention scale, distribution and placement strongly affect catchment-scale performance. — [Exeter thesis PDF](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/10871/36837/2/WebberJ.pdf)
- Dobkowitz et al. (2026), HESS 30, 3041-3060 (SWMM plus TELEMAC-2D at 2 m, 3.3 km2 of Berlin): bioretention gave the highest flooded-area reduction per square metre of measure; the paper does not analyse placement. — [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3041/2026/)

### Inferences
- Closest prior work to claim (i), location matters more than size: Rehman et al. 2024 (many small zones beat few large ones on cost-benefit, in a 2D model) and Vercruysse et al. 2019 (sources of flooding are spatially specific and differ by criterion). Neither, as far as the abstracts show, states a location-to-size ratio, and both work with zones or source cells much coarser than a rain garden. The qualitative claim "placement matters" is well established; a quantified "location dominates size at patch scale" was not found.
- Closest prior work to claim (iii), verification by intervention in a solver: the Newcastle source-to-impact method is itself an intervention experiment in CityCAT (change rainfall or surface at a source cell, observe the change at receptors). Verifying a placement by re-running a 2D solver is standard practice and is not novel in itself. What may differ is using the solver only to check a terrain-derived ranking, not to generate it.
- The upstream-versus-downstream evidence conflicts (uniform, midstream, upstream, near-outlet and near-flood placements each win somewhere). The outcome depends on storm, catchment form, storage exhaustion and the objective. A general rule should not be claimed from one London site.

### Gaps
- Size of the rainfall source cells and number of CityCAT runs in Vercruysse et al. 2019 and Iliadis et al.: not recovered. This matters for the "almost no computational cost" comparison; the team should read both papers.
- I did not find any study with intervention units of a few square metres at 1 m resolution. Four of the 20 searches targeted this directly. Absence is not established.
- Whether any paper reports that sources and flooded locations do not coincide in numbers: not recovered.

## 3. Is depression filling, fill-and-spill, bluespot or flow-path analysis used to choose SuDS locations?

### Takeaway
Yes, in practice and in tools, but mostly as hazard screening and site-context mapping, not as a ranked, verified choice of intervention location. Fill-and-spill screening models exist in the peer-reviewed literature (Arc-Malstrøm, Safer_RAIN) and commercially (SCALGO Live), and UK standards already tell designers to follow natural flow paths and exceedance routes. I found no paper that derives a per-location benefit ranking for absorbing patches from a depression hierarchy and then tests it in a hydrodynamic model.

### Cited Findings
- Balstrøm and Crawford (2018), "Arc-Malstrøm: A 1D hydrologic screening method for stormwater assessments based on geometric networks", Computers & Geosciences 116, 64-73. Screens a high-resolution terrain model for local sinks, their capacities and contributing catchments, and routes spill between them on a 1D network with no hydrodynamics; presented as a quick first overview. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016%2FJ.CAGEO.2018.04.010); [University of Copenhagen note](https://ign.ku.dk/english/climaccess/pdf/Arc-Malstroem.pdf)
- The open-source malstroem tools implement this method; developed by Septima and Thomas Balstrøm for the Danish Agency for Data Supply and Efficiency. — [GitHub SDFIdk/malstroem](https://github.com/SDFIdk/malstroem)
- Samela, Persiano, Bagli et al. (2020), "Safer_RAIN: A DEM-based hierarchical filling-and-spilling algorithm for pluvial flood hazard assessment and mapping across large urban areas", Water 12(6), 1514. Fill-and-spill with spatially distributed rainfall and Green-Ampt infiltration per pixel; two case studies (Lignano Sabbiadoro, Rimini); against a 2D model, sensitivity 71% and specificity 99%. Keywords include nature-based solutions. — [Water 12, 1514](https://mdpi.com/2073-4441/12/6/1514)
- Esri's bluespot workflow (identify depressions, build nodes and edges, compute spillover and fill events) follows the Danish Road Directorate bluespot concept. — [Esri Learn](https://learn.arcgis.com/en/projects/model-bluespots-to-map-flood-risk/)
- Danish practice uses a tiered screening: depressions first, then the most at-risk ones, then catchment area and drainage capacity. — [Klimatilpasning](https://en.klimatilpasning.dk/cases/items/new-model-identifies-vulnerable-areas)
- SCALGO Live (vendor material, November 2025) states that its terrain-based modelling identifies overland flow routes and exceedance corridors before detailed hydraulic design and maps flow paths, depression storage and infiltration capacity for SuDS compliance in Great Britain. Promotional source. — [SCALGO blog](https://scalgo.com/pl/blog/four-ways-to-demonstrate-suds-compliance-with-scalgo-live)
- A Politecnico di Torino master's thesis used SCALGO Live with the Curve Number method to assess four nature-based solutions in a Danish catchment. Not peer reviewed. — [Thesis PDF](https://webthesis.biblio.polito.it/37120/1/tesi.pdf)
- Huang et al. (2019), "A Depression-Based Index to Represent Topographic Control in Urban Pluvial Flooding", Water 11(10), 2115: pluvial flooding occurs only within depressions; a Topographic Control Index ranks them. A Beaumont, Texas application found 74 medium-or-high index depressions as priority hotspots, about 84% overlapping the two-year inundation map. Used for hotspot ranking, not for siting measures. — [Water 11, 2115](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/11/10/2115); [OSTI record](https://www.osti.gov/pages/biblio/3024421-identifying-urban-pluvial-frequency-flooding-hotspots-using-topographic-control-index-remote-sensing-radar-images-early-warning-systems)
- A comparison study benchmarked fill-spill-merge and the topographic wetness index against a 2D hydrodynamic model for flood-prone area mapping. Citation not identified (search summary only). — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373011381_Towards_urban_pluvial_flood_mapping_using_data-driven_models)
- Webber, Gibson, Chen, Savic, Fu and Butler (2018), "Rapid assessment of surface-water flood-management options in urban catchments", Urban Water Journal 15(3), 210-217: fast screening of 12 high-level intervention strategies with the CADDIES cellular-automata model and simplified intervention representations. — [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/31593)
- Webber et al. (2021), "Towards Regional Scale Stormwater Flood Management Strategies through Rapid Preliminary Intervention Screening", Water 13, 2027: CADDIES plus the OpenProFIA loss model across about 8,300 km2 of the San Francisco Bay Area. — [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/126563?show=full)
- Li, Uyttenhove and Van Eetvelde (2020), Landscape and Urban Planning 194: GIS multi-criteria priority areas for green infrastructure in Ghent with AHP weights over five criteria (runoff mitigation, vulnerable groups, flood-sensitive roads and buildings, environmental justice). No hydrodynamics. — [Roskilde record](https://forskning.ruc.dk/en/publications/planning-green-infrastructure-to-mitigate-urban-surface-water-flo/)
- England's national SuDS standards say conveyance and exceedance routes should follow the site's natural drainage paths so exceedance flows can be captured downstream. — [GOV.UK national standards for SuDS](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-standards-for-sustainable-drainage-systems/national-standards-for-sustainable-drainage-systems-suds)
- UK councils refer designers to CIRIA C635 "Designing for exceedance in urban drainage" for exceedance route design. — [Gloucestershire County Council](https://gloucestershire.gov.uk/planning-and-environment/flood-risk-management/flooding-information/information-for-developers/surface-water-drainage-and-major-planning-applications/guidance-to-local-planning-authorities-on-development-and-flood-risks/designing-for-exceedance)
- Arcadis has worked with London on calculating SuDS return on investment (vendor blog; content not read). — [Autodesk blog](https://www.autodesk.com/blogs/water/2023/01/11/london-wants-more-suds-heres-how-arcadis-is-helping-them-calculate-suds-roi/)

### Inferences
- Closest prior work to claim (ii), best locations from depression and flow-path structure at near-zero cost: Arc-Malstrøm/malstroem and SCALGO Live (the same fill-spill network on LiDAR terrain, used to see where water comes from and goes) and Safer_RAIN (fill-and-spill with infiltration, validated against 2D). These establish that the fast terrain model and its use for screening are not new. What I did not find is a quantitative ranking of candidate absorbing locations from that structure, with benefit measured at receptors and checked against a 2D solver.
- The team should phrase claim (ii) as an extension of bluespot screening practice, and should expect reviewers from Denmark and UK consultancy to say SCALGO users already do this by eye.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study using SCALGO Live or malstroem specifically to rank SuDS sites was found (two targeted searches). Grey literature and consultancy reports probably exist; I did not find them.
- SuDS Studio, GI-Val, HR Wallingford tools, the Blue-Green Cities and Urban Flood Resilience project outputs: not searched individually. No finding either way.
- CIRIA C753 and C635 own text, the London Sustainable Drainage Action Plan and TfL SuDS guidance: not retrieved. One search returned only council guidance citing them.
- Full text of Webber et al. 2018 (resolution, the 12 strategies, run times, results by return period): blocked.

## 4. Does a small fraction of area give most of the benefit; targeted versus random ratios?

### Takeaway
I found no published, quantified ratio of flood benefit for targeted versus random placement of SuDS, and no "x% of area gives y% of benefit" figure for urban pluvial flooding. The idea that contributing areas are concentrated is supported qualitatively by the Newcastle source-to-impact work and by a 2025 conference abstract on catchment flood source areas.

### Cited Findings
- Weiler and Kirn (EGU 2025 abstract, not peer reviewed; catchment-scale, not urban street-scale): a flood-source-area index exceeds 1 on 10-60% of catchment area and equals zero on 15-90% of area, depending on event and catchment; source areas "can occur anywhere in a catchment". — [EGU25-17281](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-17281.html)
- Vercruysse et al. (2019): locations differ in their contribution to flood extent, depth and exposure, and the ranking depends on the criterion. No percentages in the abstract. — [White Rose repository record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/149868/)
- Dawson et al. (2025), Journal of Flood Risk Management, "Practitioner perspectives of Flood Source Area analysis for system-based..." links flood source areas to strategic prioritisation of mitigation measures. Content not read. — [White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/231608/8/J%20Flood%20Risk%20Management%20-%202025%20-%20Dawson%20-%20Practitioner%20Perspectives%20of%20Flood%20Source%20Area%20%20FSA%20%20Analysis%20for%20System%E2%80%90Based.pdf)
- Dobkowitz et al. (2026): reduction per square metre of measure differs by type (bioretention highest) and peaks for 30-43 mm one-hour events; total green infrastructure area in scenarios was 3.4% to 36.0% of the catchment. This is efficiency by measure type, not by location. — [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3041/2026/)
- Equal-area SWMM comparison: uniform placement beat upstream, centre and downstream clustering (see section 2). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-021-03036-6)

### Inferences
- A targeted-versus-random benefit ratio at patch scale appears to be an open quantity in what I could reach. If the team reports one, it should be stated for its site, storm and metric, since the equal-area studies show the ranking of strategies changes with these.
- The uniform-beats-clustered result is a caution: in pipe-network models, spreading the same area can outperform concentrating it. This is in tension with a strong "location over size" claim and should be addressed directly.

### Gaps
- Targeted versus random placement ratio: nothing found in 3 searches that included those terms. Not conclusive.
- Percentages from the Newcastle flood-source-area papers: not recovered; they may contain exactly this kind of figure.

## 5. Has anyone computed adjoint or automatic-differentiation sensitivity of flood depth at a building to infiltration or roughness at every other cell?

### Takeaway
Differentiable and adjoint shallow-water models exist and have produced per-cell sensitivity maps for friction in urban flood settings, and adjoint sensitivity to infiltration exists for channel and overland-flow models. I found no study that uses such gradients to place green infrastructure or that reports sensitivity of depth at a building to infiltration at every cell.

### Cited Findings
- Pujol, Garambois, Delenne and Perrin (2024 preprint, HAL): a differentiable 2D shallow-water model giving high-resolution sensitivity maps of local gradients and derivative-based global sensitivity measures, used to calibrate friction for urban flood modelling. Working paper; page could not be opened, details from search summary. — [HAL hal-04643149](https://hal.umontpellier.fr/IMAG-MONTPELLIER/hal-04643149v1)
- Castaings et al. (2007), HESS Discussions: adjoint sensitivity analysis of a distributed model with kinematic-wave overland flow and Green-Ampt infiltration, giving spatial parameter sensitivities of the hydrological response. — [HESSD 4, 363](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/4/363/2007/hessd-4-363-2007.pdf)
- Elhanafy and Copeland: adjoint sensitivity of predicted water levels to inflow, topography, friction and infiltration rate, applied to flash-flood mitigation in arid regions (channel flow). — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/id/eprint/3620)
- Tügel et al.: infiltration is the most important loss in short 2D flood events and interacts with friction (higher friction, slower flow, more infiltration). Forward model study, not adjoint. — [Environmental Modeling & Assessment](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10666-021-09788-0.pdf)

### Inferences
- The adjoint route to "which cells matter for this building" is technically available and partly demonstrated for friction, but appears unused for SuDS siting. The team's fill-spill-merge ranking is a different, cheaper route to a similar per-cell quantity. A reviewer may ask for comparison with an adjoint or AD gradient; Pujol et al. is the paper to cite.

### Gaps
- Two searches on this question (one extended). Differentiable-hydrodynamics work is moving quickly and may include green-infrastructure applications I did not find.
- Whether Pujol et al. include infiltration sensitivities: unknown.

## 6. How much do SuDS reduce flooding in extreme short storms?

### Takeaway
Modelling studies agree that relative benefit falls as rainfall depth rises, but it does not vanish: for one-hour events near the 100-year level (about 49 mm in Berlin) and for 100 mm events, catchment-wide scenarios cut flood volume or flooded area by roughly 15-35%, and these scenarios cover from a few percent to over a third of the catchment. Evidence is almost entirely simulated design storms, not monitored events.

### Cited Findings
- Dobkowitz, De Vos, Jarajapu, Lindenlaub, Samprogna Mohor, Seleem and Bronstert (2026), HESS 30, 3041-3060. SWMM plus TELEMAC-2D (2 m mesh) plus a building damage model; 3.3 km2, about 55% impervious, Berlin; one-hour storms of 14.8-48.9 mm (1 to 100-year) plus 100 mm; 70 scenarios. Across scenarios and events, relative reductions were 4.9-94.8% for runoff, 3.2-94.1% for area flooded deeper than 10 cm, and 0.9-94.6% for building damage, generally falling as rainfall increases. — [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3041/2026/)
- Same study, 100-year event (49 mm): green roofs on all buildings cut flooded area by about 30% and damaged buildings by about 14% (about 67% for medium or higher damage); extra gullies cut flooded area about 13%. At 100 mm: green roofs about 20%, gullies about 3%; building damage reduction was smallest at 100 mm. — [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3041/2026/)
- Same study: bioretention at maximum extent covers 10% of the catchment and pervious pavement 50% of roads; flooded-area reduction per square metre of measure peaked at 30-43 mm events. — [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3041/2026/)
- Earlier Berlin results cited there: Neumann et al. (2024) about 32-34% flood volume reduction for the 100-year event and about 34% for retention roofs at 100 mm; Tügel et al. (2025) about 35% from retention roofs at 106.7 mm. The authors warn that differing metrics limit comparison. — [HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3041/2026/)
- Preprint version of the same study: best combination (bioretention, green roofs, pervious pavement) cut flood volume 33.5% for a 100 mm one-hour event; in an average rainfall year the scenarios cut peak runoff 31-48%. — [EGUsphere preprint](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-5466/egusphere-2025-5466-manuscript-version2.pdf)
- A 2024 Water study: mitigation by nature-based solutions declines with rainfall intensity; infiltration systems dimensioned for a 100-year event achieve almost three times the flood reduction of 5-year designs, cutting total flood volume 15.1-25.8% even in a 100 mm event. It also notes many guidelines size infiltration features for 2 to 10-year storms. — [Water 16(6), 811](https://mdpi.com/2073-4441/16/6/811/html)
- A Swedish study cites findings that blue-green infrastructure effectiveness does not scale proportionally with storm size, that detention-based facilities did better in larger storms (114 and 279 mm) than smaller ones (18 and 45 mm), and that centralised facilities near the outlet sometimes raised peak discharge. — [SLU publication](https://pub.epsilon.slu.se/id/document/20439821)
- A Melbourne application of the CADDIES screening approach found intensive green infrastructure could substantially reduce depth and velocity but residual risk remains, particularly in extreme events. Citation details not confirmed. — [Urban Water Journal listing](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1573062X.2019.1700286)
- Webber's thesis argues that interventions with "resilient properties" such as green infrastructure do not necessarily deliver resilient performance, which must be tested across many rainfall scenarios. — [Exeter thesis PDF](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/10871/36837/2/WebberJ.pdf)
- UK design context: systems are typically designed to the 1-in-100-year event plus climate change allowance, with exceedance beyond that routed safely; residual risk from exceedance or blockage is acknowledged. — [Surrey County Council](https://surreycc.gov.uk/land-planning-and-development/healthy-streets/requirements-and-guidance/section?id=8.5)
- The National Infrastructure Commission's surface water study states that even with SuDS and other measures surface water flooding is likely to continue. — [NIC / Sayers and Partners](https://nic.org.uk/nic-sayers-and-partners-surface-water-future-risk-and-investment)

### Inferences
- The quantified extreme-storm benefits (15-35%) come from scenarios that convert large fractions of the catchment. A claim that a small, well-placed patch gives a large benefit in a short extreme storm would be well outside this evidence and needs its own volume balance: a patch can only absorb what reaches it and what its storage and infiltration rate allow in an hour.
- Benefit per square metre peaking at mid-range storms (30-43 mm in Berlin) suggests the "location over size" result may be storm-dependent; the team should report it across return periods, as Rehman et al. found optimal layouts change with severity.

### Gaps
- No UK or London-specific quantified result for extreme one-hour storms was retrieved.
- No monitored (non-modelled) evidence for extreme events was found.
- Whether the 2024 Water study and the Neumann et al. study are the same work is not confirmed.
