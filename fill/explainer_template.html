Which bit of pavement floods a building?

A weekend experiment on half a kilometre of London around Southwark Underground station, explained for someone who has never worked on floods.

What we did

When heavy rain falls faster than the drains can take it, the water runs downhill along the street, fills the low spots and collects against buildings. Flood maps show where that water ends up. They do not show which part of the street sent it there.

We tested that directly. We split the open ground into 5 metre squares, 7,894 of them. For each square we raised it by 5 cm, about the height of a low kerb, ran the storm again in a physics simulation, and looked at whether the highest water at any building wall changed by a centimetre or more. Then we did the same with the square lowered. That is 15,788 tests.

Done one at a time, the tests would take about 55 hours of graphics-chip time on our laptops. A 5 cm change mostly affects water within about 20 metres, so squares 150 metres apart can be changed in the same run without disturbing each other. That cut the work to 1,956 runs and about 7 hours, with the checking runs included.

About one square in a hundred matters. The square is typically 20 metres from the building it affects, and some are on railway land between buildings. We then picked five squares from the results, changed all five in one run, and the water at five building walls fell by 1.8 to 6.5 cm and nowhere else rose by a centimetre.

How flood simulations work in general

A flood simulation is bookkeeping for water. The area is cut into a grid of small squares. Each square has a ground height and holds some depth of water. The program then repeats one small step, thousands of times:

  1. Add water. Rain adds a little depth to every square.
  2. Work out the flow. For each pair of neighbouring squares, compare the water surface on each side. Water flows from the higher surface to the lower one. It flows faster when the difference is bigger and the water is deeper, and slower over rough ground.
  3. Move the water. Take that much water out of one square and put it in the other. Nothing is created or lost.
  4. Advance the clock by a fraction of a second and repeat.

The equations behind step 2 are called the shallow-water equations, and every serious flood model uses some form of them. The models differ in what they leave out to run faster. The time step has to be tiny, because water must not jump more than one square per step, so deeper and faster water forces smaller steps. That is why these simulations are expensive: millions of squares, tens of thousands of steps.

The national flood maps in England are made this way on a 2 metre grid. Cheaper methods skip the flow altogether and just fill the dips in the ground like a bath. They answer in seconds, but they cannot say what happens while the water is moving.

How our simulation works, step by step

  • The grid. 512 by 512 squares of one metre, so about 262,000 squares. Each has a ground height from the Environment Agency's laser survey.
  • Buildings. Squares under buildings are walls. Water cannot enter them and flows around.
  • The storm. Rain builds up evenly for 20 minutes to a peak of about 125 mm an hour, then eases off for 20 minutes. About 43 mm falls in all. The drains are assumed to take 12 mm an hour while it rains, which leaves 35 mm on the ground. That is the "35 mm storm". The simulation runs for a full hour so the water has time to settle. We also ran storms that leave 15 mm and 70 mm.
  • The flow rule. We use a simplified form of the shallow-water equations published by Bates and colleagues in 2010 and widely used for floodplains. It keeps gravity, the slope of the water surface and friction with the ground, and drops one term that only matters in fast, steep flow.
  • The time step. It is recalculated as the water deepens, and comes out at about 0.15 seconds, so one storm is roughly 18,000 steps.
  • The edges. Water that reaches the edge of the area flows out freely.
  • The hardware. Every square is updated at once on a laptop's graphics chip. One storm takes about 13 seconds.
  • What we read off. The highest water reached at 207 points on building walls that face a street. We count a point as flooded at 10 cm.
  • A built-in check. After every run the program adds up all the water that fell, left and remains. The totals agree to about one part in 100,000.

What we changed

The simulation itself is standard. Four things about how we used it are ours.

  • We varied the ground, not the rain. The usual use of many simulations is to try many storms. We held the storm fixed and asked which ground matters, by moving every square up and down.
  • We ran many tests inside one simulation. A 5 cm change mostly affects water nearby. So we changed squares 150 metres apart in the same run, about nine at a time, and credited each change in the water to the nearest changed square. This is the step that cut 15,788 runs to 1,956.
  • We re-ran every finding apart from the others. About one time in twenty the nearest square was not the cause, because long ponds carry an effect further than we allowed for. The re-runs catch that, so they are part of the method.
  • We set pass marks before running each check, and report the ones we failed.

To make that possible the solver had three additions: it can run a different ground in each copy of the street at once, it treats buildings as walls, and it records the highest water at each wall point.

How accurate the height survey is

The ground heights come from the Environment Agency's LIDAR survey: a plane measures the ground with a laser, giving one height for every square metre.

Kind of errorStated sizeWhat it means
Absolute heightwithin ±15 cmThe official specification for how far a height can be from the true height above sea level.
Recent surveysabout ±5 cmWhat the Environment Agency says newer flights achieve.
Point to pointno more than ±5 cmHow much neighbouring points can be off relative to each other. This is the one that matters for where water flows.
Position on the map±40 cmHow far sideways a point can be.

These are best-case figures. They are checked on flat, open surfaces such as car parks. No published figure covers ground next to buildings or under trees, where the survey has to fill in. A one-metre grid also cannot reliably see a kerb, which is about 12 cm high and a few centimetres wide.

This is why the result matters. The changes we tested, 5 cm, are the same size as the survey's own error. If one square in a hundred decides a building's flooding, those are the squares worth measuring properly on foot. The other ninety-nine can be left as they are.

Drainage and trees

We have no data on the real sewers under these streets, so drainage is handled in two simple ways.

  • A flat allowance. In the main experiment, 12 mm of rain an hour disappears everywhere while it is raining. The national flood map of 2013 used the same 12 mm an hour, and the 2025 version uses 18. The Environment Agency itself calls drain capacity the largest source of uncertainty in its mapping.
  • Road drains placed by rule. As a check, we replaced the flat allowance with 303 drains, one at the lowest point of the road in every 25 metre square, together able to take the same amount of water. 79 of our 98 findings still held. The drains are invented positions, not real ones.
  • Street trees, a first look. 20 trees with soil pits, placed where water at the kerb is deepest, lowered the water at 8 building walls by 1 to 1.7 cm in the 35 mm storm. None dropped below 10 cm. Placed at random, the same trees helped one; in the 70 mm storm, none. This was one run with one placement, and the pit size is an upper limit, so treat it as a first look.

We report water depth at building walls and nothing beyond that. We do not estimate water inside buildings, damage or cost. As a guide, the Environment Agency says water over 15 cm tops a standard kerb and floods some properties, and 30 cm is likely to flood a property, because floors usually sit about 20 cm above the ground outside.

The fix was chosen for the 35 mm storm and carries over only partly to the others. Our findings also change with the storm: at double the rain, 53 of 98 held, so each storm size needs its own set of tests.

Why insurers would care

We did no insurance analysis. This section is our reading of how the result could matter, with the outside figures sourced.

  • Rain floods are a large and growing share of risk. The Environment Agency counts 4.6 million properties in England at risk from surface water, against 2.4 million from rivers and the sea. A Met Office study expects downpours of 20 mm an hour four times as often in the UK by 2080 as in the 1980s if emissions stay high.
  • The losses are real. The industry body PERILS put insured losses from the London floods of 12 to 14 July 2021 at £281 million.
  • Small local features decide claims. A broker's study of those 2021 claims found many were driven by boundary walls, blocked drains, drain backup and basements. Our result says the same thing from the simulation side: a few square metres of ground, often 20 metres away, change the water at a wall by centimetres.
  • Pricing a single property. Insurers price flood risk per address from maps built on the same height survey. If a building's result hangs on a handful of squares whose height is known only to within 5 cm, the map alone cannot tell two neighbouring buildings apart. A list of the squares that matter says where a site survey would change the answer.
  • Paying for prevention. If lowering one patch of pavement takes several centimetres off the water at a wall, that is a cheap measure an insurer or council could fund. We showed one such fix working in the simulation, on one street.

None of this is tested against claims. We have one neighbourhood, no sewer data and no record of which doors flooded. An insurer would need the method run on streets where claims are known.

The 2020 burst water main

On 21 January 2020 a 36-inch water main burst at the junction of Blackfriars Road and Union Street, beside Southwark station. London Fire Brigade sent three engines. The station closed, Blackfriars Road was shut between Southwark Street and Webber Street, and about 400 customers reported low pressure or no water. Thames Water called the repair one of the most complicated it had carried out, and the side roads stayed closed for months.

How it fits our model

  • It is a different kind of flood. The water came out of a pipe at one point, at a rate we do not know. Our model rains evenly on everything. So the burst is not a test of our results, and we have not simulated it.
  • It does confirm the lie of the land. Water from the burst collected at the crossroads and the station entrance. In our 35 mm storm the lowest stretch of Blackfriars Road, a few metres from that junction, holds 17 cm of water at its highest. At 15 mm it is 10 cm and at 70 mm it is 25 cm.
  • What a proper comparison would need. The flow from the pipe over time, and a record of how deep the water got and where. With those, the same simulation could be fed from a single point in place of rain.

What this does not show

  • One neighbourhood and mainly one storm. The squares that matter change with the storm.
  • No real sewer data. The simulation shows where water collects, and it is not a forecast.
  • No recorded rain flood here to compare against. The fire brigade logged no flooding call-out inside the area on 12 July 2021 and one, just outside it, on 25 July 2021.
  • Two checks did not finish in time and have no verdict.
  • Buildings are solid walls. The national maps let water into buildings above 30 cm.
