# Storage, spill points and survey error decide Southwark flooding

The sources support modelling every absorption intervention at this site as a finite store with slow release, not as an infiltration sink. UK design rules leave well under 3 mm of ground infiltration in a 40-minute storm, and Southwark's own flood risk assessment says SuDS here should be storage-led and lined. The team's 12 mm/h drainage allowance matches the Environment Agency's 2013 national default, but that figure was applied after cutting urban rainfall to 70%, and the 2025 national model has replaced it with 18 mm/h from 100% runoff. Buildings as solid walls and a single Manning's n of 0.015 also depart from both national methods. A 5 cm terrain error is defensible on open tarmac, where the Environment Agency measures its accuracy, but nothing found supports a 3 m correlation length: the one verified study of comparable 1 m LiDAR reports 65 to 102 m. On novelty, "rain needed before a point floods" from a depression hierarchy is prior art in Danish practice, SCALGO Live and the algorithms literature. What was not found is attribution of a per-building threshold to specific spill cells, propagation of survey error to that threshold, hierarchy-guided perturbation confirmed in a 2D solver, or targeted re-survey; each of those absences rests on only a handful of searches. The site's rainfall flood record is nuisance ponding with no published depths, so the model cannot be validated there, and 40 to 60 mm in an hour is the observed London severe case, with anything above 92 mm in an hour beyond the UK record. Much of the evidence below was read only as abstracts or search snippets, and each section says where.

## In a 40-minute storm every intervention is a finite store, not a sink

### The ground under Blackfriars Road rules out counting infiltration

The study square sits on reclaimed Thames floodplain. A geoarchaeological report for 216-220 Blackfriars Road, citing the British Geological Survey, gives London Clay bedrock under alluvium (clay, silt, sand and peat) with river terrace gravel beneath, and places the site at the margin of the Bankside Channel, a palaeochannel running from Waterloo towards Bankside. Three boreholes found alluvium and peat over gravel, and **none of five trial pits got through the made ground** ([PCA 216-220 Blackfriars Road](https://docs.planning.org.uk/20240124/54/S7O39GKB00U00/fwqejl1ciohtyqz9.pdf)). The only tabulated depths come from an analogue site about 1.5 km east, outside the square: made ground averaging 3.7 m, gravel averaging 4.4 m thick, London Clay from 7.4 to 8.7 m below ground, and **perched groundwater in the gravel at 3.4 to 4.4 m below ground** ([CGL Long Lane desk study](https://docs.planning.org.uk/20220719/54/REWRM2KB00300/8o24hfsub2lnt6sl.pdf)). Treat those depths as indicative only.

Council policy points the same way. Southwark's Strategic Flood Risk Assessment says most of the borough is "potentially unsuitable for infiltration SuDS", that river terrace areas need enhanced site investigation, and that "selected SuDS features will need to be focused on surface water storage and attenuation, and appropriately lined" ([Southwark SFRA](https://www.southwark.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2024-05/southwark_council_sfra.pdf)). The same document elsewhere calls the subsurface "potentially suitable" for bespoke infiltration designs. The two sentences face opposite ways for the same mapped class, and both mean infiltration cannot be assumed. The Surface Water Management Plan requires infiltration test pits site by site ([Southwark SWMP](https://services.southwark.gov.uk/assets/attach/280890/Southwark_SWMP_v2.0.pdf)).

National design rules then remove what little is left. CIRIA C753 counts infiltration in storage design only where subgrade permeability is at least 1e-8 m/s (0.036 mm/h), and rules out infiltrating pavements where groundwater comes within 1 m of the formation ([CIRIA C753 ch. 20](https://media.marshalls.co.uk/image/upload/v1581417015/The_SuDs_manual.pdf)). The C753 factor of safety on a measured rate is **10 where failure floods a road or damages buildings**, as quoted by Severn Trent, whose own viability threshold is 1e-6 m/s ([Severn Trent SuDS manual](https://www.stwater.co.uk/content/dam/stw/stw_buildinganddeveloping/suds/general-guidance/suds-general-design-manual.pdf)). The note-taker's arithmetic from these figures: a good measured 36 mm/h becomes a design 3.6 mm/h, or about 2.4 mm in 40 minutes per square metre of infiltrating base, and a marginal 3.6 mm/h becomes about 0.25 mm. In-situ London Clay values of roughly 3e-11 to 4.5e-8 m/s give at most about 0.1 mm, although that figure was seen only as a search snippet ([Dixon et al.](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/522530/1/Dixon%20et%20al%20Hydraulic%20Conductivity%20REVISED%20paper%20for%20QJEGH%2010%205%2018.pdf)). Ground infiltration belongs in the experiments as a sensitivity case, not a base assumption.

The arithmetic that follows from these numbers sets the realistic size of each intervention's effect. A rain garden at a 10:1 loading ratio with 150 mm of ponding absorbs the first 15 mm of rain from its catchment, and its medium at 100 mm/h passes a further 5 mm of catchment rainfall in 30 minutes if an underdrain accepts it. At a 2 to 4% area ratio, ponding alone holds only 3 to 12 mm. **A rain garden therefore takes roughly the first 5 to 20 mm of a cloudburst from the area it drains and overflows after that.** Transport for London's guide says the same in words: bioretention is "likely to be overwhelmed during heavy storms" and needs contingency flow paths ([TfL SuDS guide](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/sustainable-urban-drainage-november-2016.pdf)). A 350 mm sub-base at 30% porosity stores 105 mm of water, or about 35 mm of rain across the served area at 2:1 run-on. With run-on, the surface must pass three times the rainfall intensity, so a clogged surface at the 250 mm/h design minimum fails in a 120 mm/h burst. A 15 m3 tree pit at 26% voids holds at most about 3.9 m3, an upper bound that assumes dry voids and an inlet able to deliver the flow. No source gives an inlet capacity in litres per second, and the inlet is the likely limit in a cloudburst.

Three results should not be carried into cloudburst experiments. Grass plots in Manchester "almost totally eliminated" runoff and a tree with its pit cut runoff by up to 62%, but these were 9 m2 plots under ordinary rain ([Armson et al. 2013](https://owl.cwp.org/mdocs-posts/the-effect-of-street-trees-and-amenity-grass-on-urban-surface-water-runoff-in-manchester-uk/)). The Salford tree trench averaged 81% peak attenuation over ordinary monitored events ([GMCA IGNITION](https://www.greatermanchester-ca.gov.uk/media/4138/ignition-fact-files-suds-enabled-trees_finaldoc.pdf)). Canopy interception percentages are seasonal aggregates: a paired-catchment study found tree removal raised runoff volume 4% and left peak discharge "generally not affected" ([Selbig et al. 2022](https://fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/jrnl/2022/nrs_2022_selbig_001.pdf)). Canopy is worth about 1 mm of initial loss over canopy cells, or nothing.

Grass roughness needs the same care. The 0.15 sheet-flow value applies to very shallow flow over at most 100 ft, and the Environment Agency's 2013 mapping used only 0.03 for grassland ([RoFSW 2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)). The two differ by a factor of five, so run both.

**Not found:** any UK infiltration rate or depression storage depth for amenity grass on clay; any infiltration test or borehole inside the 512 m square; C753 chapters 18, 19 and 25 in primary text; any UK field measurement of a rain garden or tree pit in a storm rarer than 1 in 30 years.

## The team's 12 mm/h is the 2013 default applied without its 70% factor

The Environment Agency has run two national surface water models, and the team's assumptions sit between them without matching either.

| Assumption | 2013 national model | 2025 national model (NaFRA2) | Team (as recorded in the notes) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urban runoff | Rainfall reduced to 70% | 100% impervious | 100% implied |
| Drainage loss | 12 mm/h (local variants 6 and 18 or 20) | 18 mm/h default (13 to 25 locally) | 12 mm/h constant |
| Roofs | Same urban rule as every cell | Same rule on "manmade" cells | Roofs drain to sewers |
| Buildings | 0.3 m floodable upstand | 0.3 m uplift | Solid walls |
| Roads | Lowered 0.125 m | Not stated | Not stated |
| Manning's n | 0.02 tarmac, 0.03 grass, 0.1 woodland | Not public | 0.015 everywhere |
| Grid | 2 m | 2 m | 1 m |
| Storm durations | 1, 3, 6 h | 1, 6, 18 h | 15 to 60 min |
| Output filter | Hazard rating below 0.575 removed | Depth below 75 mm removed | — |

The 2013 column comes from the methodology report ([RoFSW 2019](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/842485/What-is-the-Risk-of-Flooding-from-Surface-Water-Map.pdf)); the 2025 column from the current guidance and the risk management authority Q&A ([RoFSW 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/flood-risk-maps-for-surface-water-how-to-use-the-map/risk-of-flooding-from-surface-water-understanding-and-using-the-map); [NaFRA2 Q&A](https://www.middevon.gov.uk/media/358305/rma-qa-for-nafra2-and-ncerm.pdf)).

**Drainage is the largest gap.** The 2013 text reads: "rainfall is reduced to 70% to represent infiltration, then a rainfall reduction of 12mm/hr is applied". The 12 mm/h is a national typical value from a calculated range of **5 to 54 mm/h**, and the report calls drainage capacity "the source of greatest uncertainty in the modelling". At 40 mm/h the 2013 scheme leaves 16 mm/h of net rain, the 2025 scheme 22 mm/h, and 100% runoff minus 12 mm/h leaves 28 mm/h. The team's scheme is therefore more severe than both national methods at every intensity. It sits just below the 13 to 25 mm/h range the 2025 method allows. It is defensible as a cautious choice for poor drainage, but it is not "the national method". Tonight's runs should bracket it with 6, 12, 18 and 25 mm/h. The sources disagree on the 2013 high-drainage variant: 20 mm/h in the methodology report, 18 mm/h in the Environment Agency's drainage-rate study ([SC120020](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/603660e18fa8f5480ff52477/Improving_surface_water_flood_mapping_-_estimating_local_drainage_rates_-_report.pdf)).

Routing roofs to sewers has no support in either national method, which apply one urban rule to every cell including building footprints. Sending roof runoff to the sewer on top of a blanket drainage loss counts drainage capacity twice relative to the national approach. Solid walls match the superseded 2010 method, which raised footprints by 5 m. Both later models use a 0.3 m upstand that water overtops, citing reports of water passing through buildings on steep ground. Walls will deflect more flow and pond deeper against façades than the national maps show. The 2013 model lowered road cells by 0.125 m because a 2 m grid cannot resolve kerbs. A 1 m grid does not reliably resolve them either: a 10 cm kerb drop sampled at 1 to 2 m does not create gradients above 10% ([Ozdemir et al. 2013](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/17/4015/2013/hess-17-4015-2013.pdf)). The team needs an explicit decision on road lowering.

For flooding thresholds the Environment Agency's own text gives a usable ladder. Depths under **75 mm** are removed as unlikely to affect property. At **0.15 m** flooding exceeds a standard 125 mm kerb and causes "property flooding in some areas". Floor levels sit "often by around 20cm" above outside ground. At **0.30 m** flooding "is likely to cause property flooding", based on average threshold levels ([NaFRA 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024/national-assessment-of-flood-and-coastal-erosion-risk-in-england-2024)). The 2013 hazard filter of 0.575 equals 0.15 m of still water by the report's own formula. Reporting building counts at 0.15, 0.20 and 0.30 m shows the threshold sensitivity the Environment Agency itself warns about.

**Not found:** the full feature-code to Manning's n table and the depth-varying roughness inside buildings; the NaFRA2 methods documents, which are not public, so 2025 roughness, road lowering and viaduct treatment are unconfirmed; which drainage rate was applied in Southwark; any insurer or Multi-Coloured Manual threshold; any survey of real door thresholds in SE1.

## Five centimetres holds on open tarmac; three metres of correlation has no support

The official accuracy figures are consistent once absolute and relative error are separated. The specification is **absolute vertical error below ±15 cm RMSE**, which is what the 2022 composite metadata quotes ([DTM 1 m metadata](https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/01b3ee39-da3f-47b6-83da-dc98e73a461f)). The Environment Agency's technical note adds that recent surveys achieve about **±5 cm**, that relative (random) error is expected to be **no more than ±5 cm**, and that absolute horizontal error is ±40 cm ([LIDAR FAQ v5, third-party mirror](https://www.owenboswarva.com/opendata/EA/Environment_Agency_LIDAR_Open_Data_FAQ_v5.pdf)). Quality control also requires **10 cm or less for standard deviation and random error** ([Ground truth surveys](https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/123fd228-9033-4cac-afc4-4084977dd30a)).

All of these numbers are best-case by construction. Ground truth is "a few hundred points captured on a flat, unambiguous surface such as a tarmac car park or tennis court". No published figure covers ground next to buildings or under trees, where the terrain model is interpolated after automatic classification and manual editing. The technical note itself records that a large flat roof can be classified as ground and stay in the terrain model until edited by hand. The composite merges surveys flown between 2000 and 2022 with a 25 m feathered join for 1 m data, and each tile ships with a GeoPackage of survey dates ([Composite guide](https://environment.data.gov.uk/api/file/download?fileDataSetId=b9a415e5-452d-4bbd-90ac-e2568cd2dbc5&fileName=LIDAR%20Composite%20Technical%20Information%20Guide.docx)). The team should check that file for a join crossing the square. The 2013 modellers also hand-edited about 91,000 bridge and embankment openings that the bare-earth model blocks, so every viaduct arch and underpass in the square needs inspection.

The team's 5 cm standard deviation therefore matches the stated relative error on roads and paving. It is optimistic as a blanket value, and 10 cm is the sourced candidate for building margins and tree cover. **The 3 m correlation length is unsupported.** The only fully verified variogram for comparable 1 m LiDAR comes from a vegetated Australian coast: error standard deviation 0.18 m, **correlation range 102 m, falling to 65 m** after removing land-cover and terrain effects, with the smallest errors on roads, houses and bare earth ([Leon et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4177217/)). That site overstates the magnitude for London tarmac, but its structure is the best evidence available, and the difference matters physically. Noise correlated over 3 m creates and removes small pits. Error correlated over tens of metres tilts whole street segments and can move ponding between neighbouring low points. Run at least two correlation lengths, about 3 m and several tens of metres, and report both.

Two further cautions bear on the claim that survey error moves the threshold. In a Nice river-flood study, LiDAR measurement error (uncorrelated, 0.2 m) was the smallest of three topographic uncertainty sources, with a mean Sobol index of 0.06 against 0.40 for inclusion of above-ground features ([Abily et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.07279)). And terrestrial LiDAR work in Alcester found maximum depths rose about 37% when resolution went from 1 m to 10 cm, because kerbs appear ([Ozdemir et al. 2013](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/17/4015/2013/hess-17-4015-2013.pdf)). An unresolved 12.5 cm kerb is a larger terrain error than the 5 cm noise being simulated.

**Not found:** any variogram or correlation length for Environment Agency LiDAR itself; any error statistics split between open hard ground and building margins; ground truth results for the surveys covering Southwark; how the 2022 composite treats bridges and viaducts.

## Rain-to-flood thresholds are prior art; spill-cell attribution and re-survey are not

The evidence base here is thin and uneven. Roughly half the page fetches behind this section failed, most findings rest on abstracts or search snippets, and each "not found" below rests on two to four searches. The table gives the nearest work per claim.

| Team claim | Nearest prior work | What that work does not do |
|---|---|---|
| Critical rainfall per building, set by a few square metres at a spill point | Danish Blue Spot Model level 2 (2010): millimetres of rain to fill each depression. SCALGO Live: "amount of rain required for the chosen point to flood", with spill points displayed since November 2024 | Per depression or per clicked point, not per building; no attribution to specific cells |
| Survey error moves that threshold | Lindsay and Creed (2006): Monte Carlo probability that a depression exists. Abily et al. (2016): error to depth at 20 points | Output is existence or depth, not a rainfall threshold; Abily's error is uncorrelated |
| Controlling ground from a depression hierarchy, confirmed in a 2D solver | Fill-Spill-Merge (2021) for speed; malstroem pour points; de Almeida et al. (2018) editing streets in a 2D model | Street edits were not chosen from a hierarchy |
| Deciding where to re-survey | Abily's spatial Sobol maps; adjoint topography sensitivity maps | Rank uncertainty sources or parameters; none acquires survey data |
| Exact sensitivity of depression filling | Carrière et al. (2021): persistence partial derivatives are 0 or 1 | No terrain, hydrology or volume-based threshold |
| Placement of green infrastructure matters more than area | Rehman et al. (2024); Vercruysse et al. (2019); Iliadis et al. | Zones much coarser than a rain garden; no location-to-size ratio |

**The threshold quantity itself is not new.** The Danish Road Institute's 2010 report produces a risk map "with the amount of precipitation needed to fill low-lying areas" on a 1.6 m grid over 7,500 km2 ([Blue Spot Model report](https://www.cedr.eu/download/other_public_files/research_programme/eranet_road/call_2008_climate_change/swamp/04_Report-3-The-Blue-Spot-Model.pdf)). SCALGO Live answers the same question per point and shows where each depression spills ([SCALGO documentation](https://scalgo.com/en-US/scalgo-live-documentation/analysis/flash-flood-map)). Rav, Lowe and Agarwal implement point-flood queries on a merge tree ([dblp record](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/gis/RavLA17)). Their paper could not be opened, and neither could Lowe's 2021 Duke dissertation, which reportedly extends the method to uncertain terrain with Monte Carlo ([Duke record](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/241ec42f-c64b-487a-b422-d2cc5e716df1)). **Those two unread works are the largest risk to the first two claims** and should be read before any novelty statement goes on a slide. The safe wording is that the contribution ties the known threshold to buildings, localises it to named spill cells, and measures how far those cells sit from the building they control.

Speed and decimetre sensitivity are also established. Fill-Spill-Merge handles a dataset of about 4.2 million cells in 8.7 s ([Barnes et al. 2021](https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/9/105/2021/)). De Almeida, Bates and Ozdemir found that "localised, decimetric-scale alterations in the elevation of streets can lead to remarkable differences" in inundation ([de Almeida et al. 2018](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/modeling-urban-floods-at-submeter-resolution-challenges-or-opport/)). UK exceedance practice already raises and drops kerbs to steer water ([susdrain](https://www.susdrain.org/delivering-suds/drainage-exceedance/background/background.html)). The team's distinct point is locality: which cells, how few, and how far away.

On exact sensitivity, spill elevations are death values of sublevel-set persistence, and the persistence map is a reindexing of its input whose partial derivatives are each 0 or 1 ([Carrière et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.08356)). That covers the spill-elevation part of the claim. Any novelty lies in the derivative of the volume-based rainfall threshold, a decomposition the note-taker inferred and no source states. The derivative is undefined where two candidate spill cells tie, which is exactly where the controlling cell switches. For re-survey, a meteorology result warns that the best observation targets lay "away from regions of greatest adjoint and ensemble sensitivity" ([observation targeting study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249621559_Comparing_Adjoint_and_Ensemble-Sensitivity_Analysis_with_Applications_to_Observation_Targeting), snippet only). The quantity to rank is sensitivity multiplied by prior uncertainty at each cell.

The fast model's documented blind spots transfer to the team. malstroem states that it "does not know the concept of time" and that stream capacity is infinite ([malstroem README](https://github.com/Septima/malstroem)). Safer_RAIN reached sensitivity of 71% and specificity of 99% against a 2D model ([Samela et al. 2020](https://mdpi.com/2073-4441/12/6/1514)), and its authors' group reports it underestimates extent because it has no backwater ([EGU26-13010](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-13010.html)). Bridges left in the terrain create false blue spots, the Danish report warns.

On placement, the closest competitor is CONFIGURE, which couples NSGA-II directly to the CityCAT 2D model and finds that many small permeable zones save a good share of investment and that the optimal layout changes with storm severity ([Rehman et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13989)). The Newcastle source-to-impact work ranks rainfall source cells by their contribution to flooding elsewhere ([Vercruysse et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/149868/); [Iliadis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00420)). "Placement matters" is therefore established, and confirming a placement by re-running a 2D solver is standard. A quantified location-over-size result at patch scale was not found, and neither was a targeted-versus-random benefit ratio.

The contrary evidence needs a direct answer. An equal-area comparison in a drainage-network model found uniform placement beat upstream, centre and downstream clustering ([Water Resources Management study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-021-03036-6), authors unconfirmed). Other studies favour midstream, upstream or near-outlet placement, so no general rule survives. Scale is the second constraint. In Berlin, catchment-wide scenarios covering 3.4% to 36% of the area cut flooded area by about 30% at a 49 mm one-hour storm and about 20% at 100 mm, with benefit per square metre peaking at 30 to 43 mm ([Dobkowitz et al. 2026](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3041/2026/)). A few square metres of rain garden claiming a large effect sits well outside that evidence and needs its own volume balance at each storm depth.

## The site's rainfall record is kerb ponding; London's observed severe storm is 40 to 60 mm in an hour

Flooding is documented at this exact junction, but the one large event was not rainfall. A **36-inch water main burst on 21 January 2020**, closing Blackfriars Road and Southwark station, with The Cut and Union Street shut to through traffic until mid July ([London SE1, January 2020](https://www.london-se1.co.uk/news/view/10083); [London SE1, July 2020](https://www.london-se1.co.uk/news/view/10358)). It shows that the crossroads and station entrance form a low point. It is not a validation case.

The rainfall record is minor. Council records list "footway flooding at dropped kerb" at Blackfriars Road and Boundary Row on **10 May 2006**, carriageway flooding from a blocked drain in the ward the same day, an unexplained entry at 97-101 Blackfriars Road in March 2007, and a fire brigade flood call at the Old Vic on The Cut on **23 June 2016** ([Southwark SFRA Appendix E](https://www.southwark.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2024-06/appendix_e_-_historic_flooding_rev3.pdf)). St James's Park recorded 44 mm that day ([Westminster Section 19 report](https://www.westminster.gov.uk/sites/default/files/media/documents/Section%2019%20(Final%20V1.4).pdf)). **No depth is published for any incident**, and nothing was found either way for July 2021.

The site is in none of Southwark's five former Critical Drainage Areas. The 2022 plan places it in catchment S01 Waterloo, says the national mapping "does not seem to form flow paths in this Catchment" but shows pooling on highways, and counts 161, 591 and 2,083 properties at risk in the 1 in 30, 1 in 100 and 1 in 1,000 year events. Named hotspots include Burrows Mews, Colombo Street and Meymott Street, Copperfield Street, and Pocock Street and King's Bench Street. Blackfriars Road, The Cut and Union Street are not named ([Southwark SWMP](https://services.southwark.gov.uk/assets/attach/280890/Southwark_SWMP_v2.0.pdf)). Whether those hotspots fall inside the 512 m square is the note-taker's estimate from street names. A model showing shallow, patchy ponding at road low points in 20 to 45 mm storms agrees with the record. A deep continuous flow route down Blackfriars Road would contradict the council's mapping.

Radar intensities exceeded 100 mm/h over Bayswater and passed 150 mm/h in parts of Westminster, with return periods near or above 1 in 200 years ([Barbour, CIWEM](https://www.ciwem.org/assets/pdf/assets/uploads/Fiona_Barbour_paper.pdf); [Westminster Section 19 report](https://www.westminster.gov.uk/sites/default/files/media/documents/Section%2019%20(Final%20V1.4).pdf)). Gauges missed the cores and recorded about 40 to 48 mm over roughly three hours. The storm centre lay about 3 km north-west of the site.

Sources disagree on two points. Kew's 47.8 mm on 12 July is reported by one source as falling "in a single hour" ([JBA](https://www.jbarisk.com/knowledge-hub/event-response/a-retrospective-look-at-summer-2021-london-flash-floods/)), while hourly gauge data gives 40.2 mm over three hours, so the hourly reading is probably a misreported daily total. The 2004 Dulwich storm is 60 mm "in just under one hour" in one council document and over two hours in another.

The Environment Agency's climate allowances for the London management catchment add **20 to 40%** to peak rainfall ([allowances guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/peak-rainfall-climate-change-allowances-by-management-catchment)). One nearby drainage design uses 103.2 mm/h for the 1 in 100 year peak before uplift, with the duration unstated ([Vinegar Yard report](https://data.london.gov.uk/download/vql0l/28b96b8e-c46b-4031-b790-c6d063a39893/18.%2020211029-4188-Vinegar%20Yard%20Drainage%20Assessment%20Report.pdf)). The upper end of the team's 10 to 120 mm test range is therefore a stress test, not a realism check.

## Conclusion

The evidence reshapes tonight's experiments in three ways. First, absorption effects are bounded by storage arithmetic: a rain garden is worth 5 to 20 mm of rain from the area that drains to it, so its measurable effect appears only where that volume is comparable to what separates a building from its threshold. Location results should therefore be reported across storm depths from about 20 to 90 mm, because both the Berlin and Newcastle work found the answer changes with severity. Second, the drainage allowance and the error correlation length are larger levers than any intervention. Net rainfall at 40 mm/h ranges from 16 to 28 mm/h across defensible drainage schemes, and the published correlation range is twenty to thirty times the team's. Any headline about a threshold moving by tens of percent needs to survive both sweeps, and needs an answer to the Nice finding that measurement error ranked last.

Third, the defensible novelty is narrower and more specific than "critical rainfall per building". It is the chain from a known threshold quantity to named spill cells, to an error distribution on that threshold, to a causal check in a 2D solver, to a survey priority weighted by local uncertainty. No source found does any link past the first, but that absence rests on a few searches and two unread works by Lowe and co-authors. The site offers no depth observation to validate against. The honest framing is a method demonstrated on real terrain and checked for consistency with a record of kerb-level ponding, not a calibrated prediction for Southwark station.
